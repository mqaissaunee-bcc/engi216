Orientation

How to use this problem session

Every example in Chapter 1 asks the same core question in a new disguise: how many independent inputs does it take to fully control this device? That number is its mobility, and one compact formula answers it. Today we apply that formula twelve times, watch each mechanism move, and find the two places the formula quietly lies.

Each example works the same way

  • Watch first. Press Play on the animated skeleton to see the motion before you count anything — your eyes should agree with the arithmetic at the end.
  • Step through the reasoning. Use Next to advance one logical step at a time: identify the links, count the joints, substitute, interpret. Good for working it on the board together.
  • Reveal the answer. On the last step, the boxed result and its physical meaning appear. Try to predict it before you reveal.

Everything runs offline in a single file — safe to embed in Canvas, project from a laptop with no Wi-Fi, or hand to students to replay.

Analogy — the TV remote

Mobility is the number of buttons you must press to put a system in exactly the pose you want. A door (one hinge) needs one button: its angle. A robot arm with three joints needs three. If a device needs zero independent inputs to be fully defined, it cannot move at all — it is a structure, not a mechanism.

The one equation behind all twelve
F = 3(N − 1) − 2P1 − P2

N = number of links (count the ground as one). P1 = lower pairs (1-DOF joints: pins and sliders). P2 = higher pairs (2-DOF joints: a slipping point or line contact). This is the planar Kutzbach–Gruebler criterion; Stanišić writes it exactly as above.

Foundations · the four rules that decide every count

Before you count: links, pairs, and the traps

The formula is trivial arithmetic. The skill is the counting, and four recurring decisions account for nearly every wrong answer students give. Each one shows up in the examples below.

Where the numbers come from

Each term in Gruebler’s equation, and the physical idea behind it
SymbolCountsWhy that coefficient
3(N−1)Total freedom of all moving linksA free rigid body in a plane has 3 DOF (x, y, rotation). We subtract 1 link because the ground is fixed — it contributes no freedom.
−2P1Lower pairs (pin, slider)A 1-DOF joint removes 2 of the 3 freedoms between the two links it connects (it allows one motion, forbids two).
−P2Higher pairs (slipping contact)A 2-DOF joint allows two relative motions (roll and slip), so it removes only 1 freedom.

Trap 1 — the hash mark decides P₁ vs P₂

A point contact with a hatch mark (✶) is a rolling joint → lower pair, P1. The same picture without the mark is a slipping joint → higher pair, P2. Example 1.9 (rolling) and Example 1.11 (slipping) differ by nothing but that mark.

Trap 2 — multi-link pins count more than once

When three or more links share one pin, it is not one joint. Rule: a pin joining k links counts as k − 1 pin joints. A triple pin (3 links) = 2 lower pairs. This single rule is the difference between right and wrong in Examples 1.6 and 1.10.

Analogy — a group handshake

If two people shake hands, that’s one handshake. If three people all clasp the same grip, how many pairwise connections is that? Three people sharing one pin behave like two independent pin joints, because it takes two pairings to tie all three together. That’s the k−1 rule.

Trap 3 — what counts as a “link”

The ground/frame is one link, no matter how many separate mounting points it has. Two pillow blocks bolted to the same bench are one ground link. And a part that only translates (a slider block) is still a full link with its own potential 3 DOF before its joints constrain it.

Trap 4 — F is a prediction, not always the truth

Gruebler counts joints but is blind to geometry. When links have special proportions (parallel bars, equal-radius gears, a redundant support), the real mobility M can differ from the computed F. We meet this head-on in Group E (Examples 1.13 and 1.14). When that happens, trust the freezing test, not the formula.

Why an engineer cares about this number

Mobility equals the number of independent actuators (motors, cylinders, hands) you must supply to control a machine. F = 1 means one motor fully drives it — cheap, predictable, the workhorse of industry (engines, pumps, wipers). F = 2 buys richer motion (the elliptical trainer that morphs between stair-stepping and striding) at the cost of a second input. F ≤ 0 means you have accidentally designed a structure — useful for a bridge, useless for a linkage.

Worked examples · 1.2 – 1.5

Group 1 — pins only: open and closed chains

We start where every count is unambiguous: mechanisms built from links and pin joints alone, with no sliders and no contacts. These three establish the rhythm — count links, count pins, substitute — and show the difference between an open chain that needs many drivers and a closed loop that needs just one.

Example 1.2

A planar robot arm

A planar robot arm has a base link bolted to the floor and three serial segments, each joined to the next by a pin. Find the theoretical degrees of freedom.

3 joints rotate independently

Schematic skeleton — illustrative motion of all three joints.

Step 1 · Count the links

The base is the ground link. Add the three arm segments. N = 4 (ground + 3 moving links).

Step 2 · Count the joints

Every connection here is a simple pin between exactly two links: base–1, 1–2, 2–3. P₁ = 3. No sliding or rolling contacts, so P₂ = 0.

Step 3 · Substitute
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(3) − 0

= 9 − 6 = 3. Predict the result, then reveal.

Answer — F = 3
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(3) − 0 = 3

An open serial chain. You must specify the rotation at each of the three pins to fix the arm’s pose — so it needs three motors. That is exactly why a basic articulated robot arm carries one actuator per joint.

1 / 3
Analogy — your own arm

Shoulder, elbow, wrist: three joints you can angle independently. To touch a precise point you set all three. An open chain “spends” one degree of freedom per pin because nothing loops back to constrain it.

Where you see F = 3

SCARA and articulated pick-and-place robots, desk lamps, and excavator arms are open chains: each added joint adds a degree of freedom and a motor or cylinder to command it.

Example 1.4

An automobile hood linkage

A car hood is raised by a six-bar linkage that keeps the hood clear of the fender as it swings up. Count its degrees of freedom.

hood swings open/closed

Schematic skeleton — single-input opening motion.

Step 1 · Count the links

Six rigid pieces make up this linkage, the car body counting as the single ground link. N = 6.

Step 2 · Count the joints

All connections are pins — seven of them — and every pin joins exactly two links. P₁ = 7, P₂ = 0.

Step 3 · Substitute
F = 3(6 − 1) − 2(7) − 0

= 15 − 14. What does that leave?

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(6 − 1) − 2(7) − 0 = 1

One degree of freedom: lifting the hood sets one angle, and the whole linkage follows. A single gas strut (or your hand) fully controls it — no second input required.

1 / 3
Analogy — a folding ironing board

Many linked bars, but one motion: you lift, and every bar moves in lockstep to a determined pose. More links did not add freedom because the loop constrains them.

Why six bars instead of one hinge?

A plain hinge would swing the hood’s back edge into the windshield. Designers add links to shape the path while keeping mobility at 1 — complex motion, single input.

Example 1.5

The four-bar linkage

The most important mechanism in the course: four links joined in a closed loop by four pins (one link is the ground). Find F.

crank drives the loop

Schematic skeleton — one input angle drives all links.

Step 1 · Count the links

Ground, crank, coupler, rocker — four links in a closed loop. N = 4.

Step 2 · Count the joints

Four pins close the loop, each joining two links. P₁ = 4, P₂ = 0.

Step 3 · Substitute
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) − 0

= 9 − 8. Note: same N as the robot arm, but one more joint.

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) − 0 = 1

Closing the chain into a loop added one joint, which cost two degrees of freedom — dropping the open arm’s F = 3 down to F = 1. Specify the crank angle and the coupler and rocker positions are fixed. This single-input loop is the backbone of half the mechanisms you will ever analyze.

1 / 3
Analogy — open hand vs. clasped hands

An open three-segment chain flops freely (F = 3). Join the far end back to the frame — clasp the loop — and suddenly one motion drives everything (F = 1). Closing a loop is how engineers tame freedom.

The four-bar is everywhere

Windshield wipers, locking pliers, bicycle brakes, the parallel-bar lamp, oil-well pump jacks, and the suspension on your car all hide a four-bar. Learn this one cold.

Worked examples · 1.6 – 1.8

Group 2 — sliders, shared pins, and the first higher pair

Now the counting gets interesting. A sliding joint is still a lower pair, but a pin shared by three links is two joints (Trap 2), and a point contact with no hash mark is the first higher pair we meet (Trap 1).

Example 1.6

A small front loader

A compact front-end loader lifts and tilts its shovel using two hydraulic cylinders. The skeleton has nine links; one pin near the shovel joins three links at once. Find F.

two cylinders extend independently

Schematic skeleton — two independent slider motions.

Step 1 · Count the links

The skeleton diagram shows nine rigid pieces, the loader body as ground. N = 9.

Step 2 · Count the lower pairs — carefully

There are nine pin joints, but watch the pin where links 6, 7 and 8 meet: three links on one pin counts as two pins (the k−1 rule). Add the two sliding joints inside the hydraulic cylinders. Total P₁ = 11.

Step 3 · Higher pairs

No rolling or slipping point contacts anywhere. P₂ = 0.

Step 4 · Substitute
F = 3(9 − 1) − 2(11) − 0

= 24 − 22. How many cylinders did the photo show?

Answer — F = 2
F = 3(9 − 1) − 2(11) − 0 = 2

Two degrees of freedom — matching the two hydraulic cylinders. One sets the boom height, the other the shovel tilt; together they place the bucket anywhere in its workspace. Miss the triple-pin rule and you would compute F = 3 and expect a phantom third cylinder.

1 / 4
Analogy — two joysticks

An F = 2 machine is like a claw game: one stick for height, one for tilt. Two independent inputs, infinitely many combined motions. That extra degree of freedom is what lets the operator both lift and dump.

Count the actuators to check yourself

On real construction equipment, F should equal the number of independent hydraulic circuits. If your formula and the cylinder count disagree, you miscounted a joint — almost always a shared pin.

Example 1.7

A folding chair (first higher pair)

A folding chair lies on its side. A pin at the end of the seat-support leg rides in a slot in the seat — a point contact with no hash mark, so it can slide. Treat the frame as ground and find the mobility.

pin slides in the slot as it folds

Schematic skeleton — the slot contact rolls and slips.

Step 1 · Count the links

For this to be a mechanism one link must be fixed — call it the frame (ground). With the seat and the support link, N = 3.

Step 2 · Lower pairs

Two ordinary pin joints. P₁ = 2.

Step 3 · The contact — hash mark or not?

The pin at the end of the support rides in a slot. There is no hatch mark, so it can both roll and slide: it is a slipping joint, a higher pair. P₂ = 1.

Step 4 · Substitute
F = 3(3 − 1) − 2(2) − 1

= 6 − 4 − 1.

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(3 − 1) − 2(2) − 1 = 1

The higher pair subtracts only one degree of freedom (not two), because a slipping contact still permits two relative motions. One input — the fold angle — opens and closes the chair.

1 / 4
The crucial half-point

A pin or slider removes 2 freedoms (coefficient 2P₁). A slipping contact removes only 1 (coefficient 1·P₂). That asymmetry — written right into the equation — is the whole reason we separate lower and higher pairs.

Analogy — a bead on a wire vs. a pin in a hole

A pin in a matched hole can only spin (1 freedom allowed, so 2 removed). A bead free to slide along a wire while also turning has more wiggle room (2 freedoms allowed, so only 1 removed). The slot contact is the bead-on-a-wire case.

Example 1.8

A racquetball-court door closer

Viewed from above, the self-closing door at the top of a racquetball court is an inverted crank-slider: three pins and one sliding joint. Find F.

door swings; link slides in block

Schematic skeleton — the slider is a lower pair.

Step 1 · Count the links

Door, connecting link, sliding link, and the wall as ground. N = 4.

Step 2 · Count the joints

Three pin joints plus one sliding joint — and a slider is a lower pair, just like a pin. P₁ = 4, P₂ = 0.

Step 3 · Substitute
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) − 0

= 9 − 8. Notice this matches the four-bar — swap one pin for a slider and F is unchanged.

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) − 0 = 1

One degree of freedom. Because a slider and a pin each remove two freedoms, a crank-slider and a four-bar have identical mobility. The door’s single closing motion is fully set by one angle.

1 / 3
Analogy — a desk drawer on a slide

A drawer can only go in or out: the slide allows one motion and forbids two, exactly like a pin allows only rotation. Same constraint budget, different geometry.

Crank-sliders run the world

Every piston engine and compressor is a crank-slider converting rotation to straight-line motion (or back). “Inverted” just means we picked a different link to be the ground.

Worked examples · 1.9 – 1.11

Group 3 — rolling vs. slipping, and two-DOF machines

Three exercise-machine and tool problems that hinge entirely on reading contacts correctly. The same Precor product line gives a one-DOF and a two-DOF machine; a clamping tool shows how two slipping contacts together behave like a single pin.

Example 1.9

Precor EFX elliptical trainer

An elliptical cross-trainer. One point contact carries a hash mark, indicating a rolling joint. Find the mobility.

single drive → elliptical foot path

Schematic skeleton — hash mark = rolling = lower pair.

Step 1 · Count the links

The skeleton has six links, the frame as ground. N = 6.

Step 2 · Lower pairs (don’t miss the hidden pin)

Six pin joints — including the easy-to-miss pin between links 3 and 4. The point contact between 4 and the frame has a hash mark, so it rolls without slipping: that is also a lower pair. P₁ = 7.

Step 3 · Higher pairs

The only contact is the rolling one, already counted as P₁. P₂ = 0.

Step 4 · Substitute
F = 3(6 − 1) − 2(7) − 0

= 15 − 14.

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(6 − 1) − 2(7) − 0 = 1

One degree of freedom. The angle of the drive arm fixes everything, so the foot follows one fixed elliptical path. The hash mark mattered: had it been a slipping contact (P₂), the count would change.

1 / 4
Analogy — a train wheel on a rail

A wheel rolling without slipping is locked to the rail at the contact: its speed is tied to its spin. That coupling is why a rolling joint behaves like a 1-DOF lower pair, not a free 2-DOF slip.

One DOF = one fixed workout path

Because F = 1, the EFX gives every user the same stride shape. Contrast that with the next machine.

Example 1.10

Precor AMT adaptive trainer

The Adaptive Motion Trainer lets the user shift smoothly from stair-stepping to long striding. Its skeleton has seven links and a pin shared by three links. Find F — and explain why the motion feels so different from the EFX.

stride morphs: stepping ↔ striding

Schematic skeleton — two independent motions.

Step 1 · Count the links

Seven rigid links, frame as ground. N = 7.

Step 2 · Lower pairs (mind the triple pin)

There are eight pin joints — because the pin connecting links 1, 4 and 7 joins three links and counts as two. P₁ = 8, P₂ = 0.

Step 3 · Substitute
F = 3(7 − 1) − 2(8) − 0

= 18 − 16.

Answer — F = 2
F = 3(7 − 1) − 2(8) − 0 = 2

Two degrees of freedom — two independent inputs: the angle of the handle (link 7) relative to ground, and the angle of link 4. Because two inputs vary freely, the stride is no longer one fixed path but a whole family ranging from short stepping to long striding. That is the machine’s entire selling point, and it falls straight out of F = 2.

1 / 3
Analogy — one DOF draws a line, two DOF fill a region

An F = 1 machine traces a single curve, like a pen on a track. Add a degree of freedom and the pen can reach a whole 2-D area — infinitely many paths. The AMT’s second DOF is what lets a runner and a stair-climber share one machine.

EFX vs. AMT, side by side

Same manufacturer, one joint of difference in topology, and the mobility jumps from 1 to 2. Reading the skeleton correctly — especially that triple pin — is what separates the two answers.

Example 1.11

A clamping tool (pin-joint equivalency)

A clamping tool’s curved jaw makes two point contacts against the frame, neither hash-marked. Find the mobility — and notice what two slipping contacts add up to.

jaw pivots on two slipping contacts

Schematic skeleton — two P₂ contacts ≈ one pin.

Step 1 · Count the links

Four links, frame as ground. N = 4.

Step 2 · Lower pairs

Three ordinary pin joints. P₁ = 3.

Step 3 · Higher pairs — count both contacts

The jaw touches the frame at two separate points, and neither has a hash mark, so both are slipping joints. P₂ = 2.

Step 4 · Substitute
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(3) − 2

= 9 − 6 − 2.

Answer — F = 1
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(3) − 2 = 1

Two higher pairs remove 2 freedoms total — the same as one lower pair. So the two slipping contacts together behave like a single pin joint: pin-joint equivalency. The tool clamps with one squeezing input.

1 / 4
Analogy — two fingers vs. one knuckle

Press one fingertip on a marble and it can roll and slide (a higher pair). Press two fingertips on it from different sides and it is pinned in place — the two soft contacts together act like one rigid joint. That is why 2×P₂ can replace one pin.

Designers use this on purpose

Cam mechanisms, ball detents, and self-centering chucks rely on multiple contacts to constrain a part without a machined pin — cheaper to make, and the mobility math still works out.

Worked examples · 1.13 – 1.15

Group 4 — where Gruebler lies, and how to check it

The formula counts joints but never looks at geometry. Two examples show it giving the wrong number, and a third introduces the freezing test — a count-free way to find the true mobility M and confirm a Gruebler result you trust.

Example 1.13

A statically indeterminate structure (F < 0)

Determine the mobility of the over-braced structure shown. Apply Gruebler, then decide whether to believe it.

it resists — rigid

Schematic skeleton — over-constrained, no motion.

Step 1 · Count

Reading the skeleton: N = 4 links joined by five lower-pair connections (pins and sliders), P₁ = 5, P₂ = 0.

Step 2 · Substitute
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(5) − 0

= 9 − 10. The result is going to be negative.

Step 3 · Interpret the sign

A negative F cannot be a count of motions. It signals more constraints than a rigid body needs — an over-braced, statically indeterminate structure. Now check reality: can it move?

F = −1, but true mobility M = 0
F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(5) = −1  →  M = 0

The structure is already immovable, so its real mobility is M = 0. Gruebler returns −1 because it cannot see that one of the supports is redundant — it removes a constraint that was already accounted for. The formula flags “over-constrained,” but only inspection gives the true M.

1 / 3
What the sign of F really tells you

F ≥ 1: mechanism (F motions). F = 0: a just-rigid, statically determinate structure. F < 0: over-constrained / statically indeterminate — redundant supports. The number’s sign is a design alarm even when its magnitude is untrustworthy.

A bridge truss, on purpose

Civil structures are designed for F ≤ 0 — you want a bridge that does not move. Redundant members (F < 0) even add safety: if one fails, others carry the load. Negative mobility is a feature there, a bug in a linkage.

Example 1.14

A pair of meshing gears (F ≠ M)

Two gears are pinned to a common frame and roll on each other (hash-marked, no slipping). Apply Gruebler — then watch the gears actually turn.

they turn — clearly M = 1

Schematic skeleton — rolling contact (hash) = lower pair.

Step 1 · Count

N = 3 (two gears + frame). Two pins fix the gear centers; the hash-marked rolling contact is a third lower pair. P₁ = 3, P₂ = 0.

Step 2 · Substitute
F = 3(3 − 1) − 2(3) − 0

= 6 − 6 = 0. Gruebler says “structure.” But press Play…

Step 3 · Reality check

The gears obviously spin — turn one and the other follows. So the true mobility is M = 1, not 0. Why the disagreement?

F = 0, but true mobility M = 1
Gruebler: F = 6 − 6 = 0  →  reality: M = 1

This is a special-geometry case. With the centers fixed at the right distance, the rolling constraint and the pin constraints are not fully independent — one is partly redundant. Gruebler counts them as if independent and so under-predicts by one. The freezing test confirms M = 1: locking any one of the three lower pairs turns the pair into a structure.

1 / 3
Analogy — counting fence posts

Gruebler counts every constraint as if it does fresh work. But when geometry makes two constraints enforce the same condition (like two fences guarding one gap), the count double-charges. The formula is bookkeeping; geometry can make two entries cancel.

The takeaway for the whole chapter

Gruebler is fast and almost always right — but it is blind to geometry. Parallel links, equal-radius rollers, concentric pivots, and meshing gears are the classic offenders. When you suspect special geometry, fall back on the freezing test.

Example 1.15

The freezing test — verifying the AMT

Re-examine the AMT trainer from Example 1.10, but this time find its mobility without the formula: freeze joints one at a time until nothing moves, and count how many you had to freeze.

moving → lock 1 → lock 2 → rigid

Schematic skeleton — each lock removes one DOF.

Step 1 · The idea

The freezing test: mobility M = the number of joints you must lock to turn the mechanism into a rigid structure. No counting of links required.

Step 2 · Lock the first pin

Lock the pin between ground (1) and link 2, fixing link 2. Now links 3 and 4 form a rigid triangle — a truss. But links 5, 6 and 7 can still move together as a four-bar. So one freedom remains.

Step 3 · Lock the second pin

Now lock the pin between links 4 and 5, fixing link 5. Links 6 and 7 form another truss, and the entire mechanism is rigid.

Step 4 · Count the locks

We froze two pins to make it immovable.

M = 2 — confirms Example 1.10
locks needed = 2  →  M = 2 = F

The freezing test gives M = 2, exactly matching Gruebler’s F = 2 from Example 1.10. When the two methods agree, you can trust the result; when they disagree (Examples 1.13 and 1.14), the freezing test wins because it actually tests motion.

1 / 4
Analogy — jamming a wobbly table

A wobbly table moves in some number of ways. Stick a wedge under one leg — one wobble gone. Keep wedging until it is solid. The number of wedges you needed is the table’s mobility. That is the freezing test in a sentence.

Two tools, one answer

In practice engineers run Gruebler first (seconds) and reach for the freezing test only when geometry looks special or the sign of F surprises them. Agreement between the two is your confidence check.

Self-check

Practice problems

New mechanisms, same tools. Work each on paper first, then tap an answer to check yourself — the feedback explains the trap.

Q1 · the engine

A single-cylinder engine is a slider-crank: ground, crank, connecting rod, and piston, joined by three pins and one sliding joint. What is its mobility?

N = 4, P₁ = 4 (3 pins + 1 slider, all lower pairs), P₂ = 0. F = 3(4−1) − 2(4) = 1. One crank angle sets the piston position — a single-DOF machine, like the four-bar.
Q2 · spot the trap

Five links are joined only by pins: four of the pins each connect two links, and one pin is shared by three links. What is F?

The shared pin joins three links, so it counts as 2 joints (k−1 rule). P₁ = 4 + 2 = 6, not 5. F = 3(5−1) − 2(6) = 12 − 12 = 0 — a structure. Counting the triple pin as one joint gives the wrong answer F = 2.
Q3 · cam & follower

A disk cam is pinned to the frame and drives a follower that translates in a guide. The follower tip touches the cam at a single point contact with no hash mark. What is F?

N = 3 (cam, follower, frame). Lower pairs: the cam’s pin and the follower’s slider → P₁ = 2. The no-hash point contact is a slipping higher pair → P₂ = 1. F = 3(3−1) − 2(2) − 1 = 1. Turning the cam fully drives the follower.
Q4 · name the higher pair

Which of these is a higher pair (P₂)?

Pins and sliders are lower pairs (remove 2 freedoms). A hash-marked rolling contact is also a lower pair. Only the meshing gear teeth, which roll and slide against each other, form a higher pair — removing just 1 freedom.
Q5 · trust, but verify

For a parallelogram linkage, Gruebler computes F = 0, yet the linkage clearly swings (M = 1). What is going on?

The parallel link lengths make one constraint redundant — a special-geometry case the formula cannot see, exactly like the meshing gears of Example 1.14. The freezing test reveals the true mobility: lock one pin and it goes rigid, so M = 1.

Reading the answer

  • F ≥ 1 — a mechanism needing F independent drivers.
  • F = 0 — a just-rigid (statically determinate) structure.
  • F < 0 — over-constrained / statically indeterminate (redundant supports).

Four counting rules

  • Hash mark = lower pair. Rolling (hatched) → P₁. Slipping (no mark) → P₂.
  • Shared pins: a pin joining k links counts as k − 1 pin joints.
  • Ground is one link regardless of how many mounts it has; a slider is still a full link.
  • F is geometry-blind. Special proportions can make the true M differ from F.

The freezing test (when in doubt)

M = the number of joints you must lock to make the mechanism rigid. Use it to confirm Gruebler, or to override it in special-geometry cases.

Reference

Glossary


Degree of freedom (DOF)
An independent motion a body or system can undergo. A free rigid body in a plane has three: two translations and one rotation.
Mobility (F or M)
The number of independent inputs (drivers) needed to fully define a mechanism’s configuration. F is the value computed by Gruebler; M is the true value found by inspection or the freezing test.
Link
A single rigid body in a mechanism. The fixed body is the ground (or frame) link and counts as one link however many mounting points it has.
Kinematic pair (joint)
A connection between two links that permits some relative motions and forbids others.
Lower pair (P₁)
A joint with surface contact that allows one relative motion and removes two freedoms: pins (revolute), sliders (prismatic), and hash-marked rolling contacts.
Higher pair (P₂)
A joint with point or line contact that allows two relative motions (roll and slip) and removes only one freedom: a slipping contact or meshing gear teeth.
Pin / revolute joint
A lower pair allowing pure rotation about a shared axis.
Slider / prismatic joint
A lower pair allowing pure translation along a line.
Rolling joint
A contact that rolls without slipping, drawn with a hash mark. It is a lower pair (P₁).
Slipping joint
A point or line contact free to roll and slide, drawn without a hash mark. It is a higher pair (P₂).
Kinematic chain
An assembly of links and joints. It becomes a mechanism once one link is fixed as ground.
Open vs. closed chain
An open chain (like a serial robot arm) has free ends and high mobility; a closed chain loops back on itself, and closing the loop removes freedoms.
Four-bar linkage
Four links in a closed loop joined by four pins. The fundamental single-DOF mechanism (F = 1).
Crank-slider
A four-link mechanism with three pins and one slider (F = 1) that converts rotation to straight-line motion, as in a piston engine.
Gruebler / Kutzbach criterion
The planar mobility formula F = 3(N−1) − 2P₁ − P₂. Kutzbach’s form includes higher pairs; Gruebler’s is the special case used for constrained single-DOF design.
Structure
An assembly that cannot move: F = 0 (just rigid) or F < 0 (over-constrained).
Statically indeterminate
An over-constrained structure (F < 0) with redundant supports, where equilibrium equations alone cannot find all internal forces.
Multi-link pin (k−1 rule)
When k links share one pin, it counts as k − 1 separate pin joints.
Pin-joint equivalency
Two slipping higher pairs acting together remove two freedoms — the same as one pin — so they behave like a single revolute joint.
Freezing test
A geometry-aware way to find mobility: M equals the number of joints that must be locked to make the mechanism rigid.
Special geometry
Link proportions (parallel bars, equal-radius gears, concentric pivots) that make constraints redundant, causing Gruebler’s F to differ from the true mobility.
