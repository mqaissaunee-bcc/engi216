Step 1 — Define the problemEquations, parameters, and guesses

💡 Analogy — the initial guess is where you drop the marble

Newton’s method is like releasing a marble onto a hilly landscape: it rolls quickly to the bottom of whichever valley you drop it in. Most engineering systems have more than one valid solution, and your initial guess decides which one you land in — the open vs. crossed assembly of a linkage, the low vs. high arc of a projectile, an amplifier’s intended operating point vs. a spurious one, or the two places a circle and a line cross. If you drop the marble on a flat ridge (a singular Jacobian), it can’t tell which way to roll and the method stalls — nudge the guess toward the answer you actually want.

Example: r2*cos(th2) + r3*cos(th3) - r4*cos(th4) - r1 = 0 · use * for every multiplication · # starts a comment

Values may use earlier names and math, e.g. th2 = deg2rad(60). Anything in the equations that is not listed here becomes an unknown.

⚠ Reserved names — don’t use these as your own variables or parameters

Constants: pi (π ≈ 3.14159), e (Euler’s number ≈ 2.71828). That is why a slider offset or orbital eccentricity is written ecc in the examples, not e.

Functions: sin cos tan asin acos atan atan2 sqrt exp ln log log10 abs sign min max sinh cosh tanh deg2rad rad2deg.

Everything else is fair game: th3, r_4, Vd, theta, E, Greek letters, etc.

    🔧 Real world — this exact loop runs everywhere

    Every time SPICE simulates a circuit, ANSYS solves a nonlinear structure, or an industrial robot computes where to put its wrist, a Newton iteration just like this one runs — often thousands of times per second. The robot’s controller solves its position equations each servo cycle and uses the previous answer as the next initial guess, which is why it converges in one or two steps.

    It didn’t converge — now what?

    Move the initial guess. The single most common fix. Sketch the mechanism (or the curves) and estimate angles within ±30°; Newton usually needs only a “same valley” guess, not a precise one.

    Singular Jacobian (det J ≈ 0)? You may be starting at a dead position or a fold of the mechanism — for a four-bar that is where links 3 and 4 line up. Nudge the guess away from it.

    Oscillating ‖F‖? Leave damping on: the solver halves the step until the residual actually decreases, trading a little speed for a lot of robustness.

    Wrong branch? The answer is valid but it’s the other assembly (crossed instead of open). Flip the sign or reflect the angle in your guess to land in the other valley.

    Check the units. All trig here is in radians. A guess of 60 meant as degrees is really 3437° — write deg2rad(60) in parameters, or 1.05 as the guess.

    How the solver works under the hood

    Each iteration evaluates the residual vector F(x), builds the Jacobian by central finite differences (each column costs two extra evaluations of F), solves J Δx = −F by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, and updates x ← x + λΔx. With damping enabled, λ starts at 1 and halves until ‖F‖ decreases. Iteration stops when ‖F‖∞ < tolerance or the step becomes negligibly small.

    The finite-difference Jacobian means you never differentiate by hand, at the cost of a few extra function evaluations per step. It is exactly the algorithm behind MATLAB’s fsolve and SciPy’s scipy.optimize.fsolve.

    💡 Analogy — finite differences are a wiggle test

    Instead of deriving ∂f/∂x with calculus, the solver wiggles each unknown a tiny amount and measures how much every equation responds — like finding which breaker controls an outlet by flipping each one and checking the lamp. Slightly slower than knowing the wiring diagram, but it always works.
