Orientation

How to use this problem session

Chapter 4 taught you to answer “how does it move?” Chapter 5 answers the question your employer actually asks: “how big a motor do I need?” That is the inverse dynamics problem — the motion is prescribed, and the forces required to produce it are unknown.

Analogy — the choreographer, not the dancer

Forward dynamics is being a dancer: you are pushed, and you find out where you end up. Inverse dynamics is being a choreographer: you have already decided exactly where every limb goes at every beat, and you are working backwards to ask how hard must each muscle pull to make that happen? The motion is the input. The forces are the answer.

Each example works the same way

Every worked example on this page has three parts, sitting side by side:

  • An animated schematic of the machine, drawn from the textbook figure. Press Play and watch the motion the driving force has to produce.
  • A step-through solution. Advance one step at a time, predict the next line before you press Next, and only then Reveal answer. Nothing is hidden from you — but the order matters.
  • Analogy and real-world callouts. The physics is the same whether the link is a windshield-wiper arm or a landing-gear strut.

Foundations

Before you draw an FBD: the six steps, and the audit that saves you

Stanišić gives one recipe and uses it for the entire chapter. Learn the recipe, and Examples 5.1 through 5.4 stop being four problems and become one problem with four costumes.

The six-step recipe (Example 5.1)

  1. Solve the position problem. Vector loop → Newton’s method → the dependent position variables.
  2. Kinematic coefficients of the mass centers. Write rgi as a chain of loop vectors, differentiate twice with respect to the input θ2 to get fgix, fgiy and their primes.
  3. Accelerations. agix = fgixθ2 + fgix′θ22, and αi = hiθ2 + hi′θ22.
  4. Inertial terms. Multiply out miagi and Igiαi. These are known numbers from here on.
  5. Free-body diagram of every moving link. Action and reaction at every former connection. Then audit: unknowns vs. equations.
  6. Force and moment equilibrium, then solve. Three scalar equations per body. Assemble A x = b and invert.

Steps 1–4 are Chapter 4. Steps 5–6 — the rust-coloured ones — are Chapter 5. Everything on this page lives there.

Where the numbers come from

The kinetic content of the whole chapter is three equations per rigid body, and they are just Newton and Euler:

ΣFx = miagix     ΣFy = miagiy     ΣMP = Igiαi + (rP→g × miagi)z

The last one is the equation students get wrong. You may sum moments about any point P fixed in the body — but if P is not the mass centre, you must add the moment of the inertial force miagi acting at Gi. In planar motion only the z component survives, so each cross product collapses to a single scalar.

Analogy — why choose a clever moment point

Summing moments about a pin is like taking a photograph from directly above a flagpole: the flagpole vanishes. Any force whose line of action passes through your moment point contributes nothing, so it disappears from that equation. Choose the pin whose reaction you care least about, and it drops out for free. In Example 5.2, summing about O for link 2 deletes both components of F12 in one stroke.

The unknown audit — a correctness check that costs ten seconds

Before writing a single equilibrium equation, count. Each moving body contributes exactly three scalar equations. Count the scalar unknowns in your FBDs. If the two numbers do not match, your free-body diagrams are wrong — you have forgotten a reaction, double-counted one, or mis-modelled a joint. Fix the picture, not the algebra.

What each connection contributes to the unknown count

Positive-drive (gear) rolling contact2Fn35 and Ft35, tied by Fn = Ft tanφ — that relation is an extra equation
Friction-drive rolling contact2Same two components, but no tooth relation. The count still closes because the clamping member carries a known preload, so its magnitude is not an unknown
Two-force member1Magnitude only; direction is the line between its two pins. Contributes zero equilibrium equations.
The thing you are solving for1Driving torque T2 or driving force magnitude F4
The linearisation trick you will use every single time

A slider reaction has unknown magnitude and unknown line of action — that gives a moment term r9F14, a product of two unknowns. The system is no longer linear. The fix: treat the product (r9F14) as a single unknown. Solve the linear system, then recover r9 = (r9F14) / F14 afterwards. Stanišić does exactly this in Example 5.1, and again with (r6F43) in Example 5.2.

Why anyone pays for this answer
  • Motor sizing. The peak of T2 over one crank revolution sets the motor and gearbox rating. Undersize it and the machine stalls at the worst crank angle; oversize it and you paid for copper you never use.
  • Bearing life. F12, F23, F34 are exactly the loads that go into an L10 bearing-life calculation. The IDP is the only way to get them.
  • Structural design. Each link must survive the pin reactions the IDP predicts — not the applied load, which is often much smaller.
  • Robot control. Computed-torque control literally runs this calculation, at every servo tick, for every joint.

Consistent units, or the factor of 32.174

Example 5.2 is worked in lbm, lbf, inches, and seconds — deliberately, because that set is not consistent. The gravitational constant gc then appears in every mass×acceleration term:

gc = 32.174 ft·lbm / (lbf·s2) = 386.09 in·lbm / (lbf·s2)

In the British Gravitational system (slug, lbf, ft, s) and in SI (kg, N, m, s), gc = 1 and disappears. Write code in a consistent system. Every place you forget gc in a non-consistent system, your answer is off by 32.174 — and in Example 5.2 that happens eleven separate times.

Analogy — currency conversion inside a spreadsheet

Working in lbm and lbf is like keeping a budget where some cells are dollars and others are euros, with no exchange-rate column. It can be done, but every arithmetic step needs a conversion you must remember to apply. Consistent units are the spreadsheet where everything is already in one currency.

Read the mismatch, do not fight it

More unknowns than equations means you are missing a relation — typically the gear-tooth condition Fn = Fttanφ, or you failed to notice a two-force member. More equations than unknowns means you dropped a reaction, or you wrote equilibrium equations for a body you had already reduced to a two-force member. Either way the picture is the bug.

This audit is a real engineering habit

Commercial multibody codes (Adams, Simscape Multibody, RecurDyn) perform exactly this count internally before assembling the constraint Jacobian, and refuse to run — or silently regularise — when it fails. Doing the count by hand is how you learn to read their error messages.

Worked example · 5.1

Group 1 — the six steps on a single loop

We begin with the example that defines the method for the whole chapter. It is deliberately symbolic: Stanišić asks you to “outline the equations you would use in a computer program,” not to produce a number. Learn the shape of the answer here, and the arithmetic in Example 5.2 becomes bookkeeping.

Example 5.1

Offset crank-slider — find the driving force

An offset crank-slider carries a known load torque T2 on link 2, and an unknown driving force F4 pushes the slider. The line of action of F4 is offset from wrist pin B by the perpendicular distance r5. Take θ2 as the input; the state of motion θ2, θ2, θ2 is known. Mass centres are located by r6, r7, r8 and the angle φ. Outline the equations that give the driving force. Neglect gravity.

Input: crank angle θ₂

Figure 5.5 redrawn — the mechanism, its vector loop, and the mass centres.

Step 1 · The position problem

One closed loop, walked from the crank pivot around to the slider:

r1 + r4 − r3 − r2 = 0

With θ1 = π/2 (the offset is vertical) and θ4 = 0 (the rail is horizontal), the two scalar components are

r4 − r3cosθ3 − r2cosθ2 = 0
r1 − r3sinθ3 − r2sinθ2 = 0

Knowns: r1, r2, r3 · Unknowns: θ2, θ3, r4 — two equations, three unknowns, so one degree of freedom. Take Si = θ2 and solve for θ3, r4 by Newton’s method.

Step 2 · Kinematic coefficients of the mass centres

Write each mass-centre position as a chain of loop vectors, then differentiate twice with respect to θ2.

rg2 = r6  ·  rg3 = r2 + r7  ·  rg4 = r1 + r4 + r8

The slider’s mass centre only translates horizontally, so fg4x = f4, fg4y = 0, and likewise the primes. That single observation kills two whole rows of arithmetic later.

Step 3 · Accelerations
agix = fgixθ2 + fgix′ θ22    α3 = h3θ2 + h3′θ22

The slider does not rotate: α4 = 0 and ag4y = 0. The crank rotates at α2 = θ2 by definition.

Step 4 · Inertial terms — now they are just numbers

Compute m2ag2x, m2ag2y, m3ag3x, m3ag3y, m4ag4x, Ig2α2, Ig3α3.

And note the two freebies: m4ag4y = 0 and Ig4α4 = 0. Everything on this list is a known constant from here on. They will all end up on the right-hand side.

Step 5 · Free bodies, then the audit

Tear the machine apart at every joint. Wherever two bodies touched, draw the action and its reaction.

  • Link 2: F12 at O, −F23 at A, plus the known load torque T2.
  • Link 3: F23 at A, −F34 at B. A slender two-pin link — but not a two-force member, because it has mass and it accelerates.
  • Link 4: F34 at B, the driving force F4 (unknown magnitude, known direction, known line of action via r5), and the ground reaction F14 — normal to the rail, so its direction is known, but its line of action is not. Locate it by r9 from B, with r9 unknown.

Unknowns: F12x, F12y, F23x, F23y, F34x, F34y, F4, F14, r9 = 9. Equations: 3 bodies × 3 = 9. The picture is correct.

Step 6a · Equilibrium, link by link

Link 2, moments about O (deletes F12):

F12x − F23x = m2ag2x
F12y − F23y = m2ag2y
r2sinθ2F23x − r2cosθ2F23y = m2r6(cosθ2ag2y − sinθ2ag2x) + Ig2α2 + T2

Link 3, moments about A (deletes F23). Link 4, moments about B (deletes F34) — and there the trouble starts.

Step 6b · The nonlinear term, and the trick

Link 4’s moment equation about B is

r5F4 + r9F14 = −m4r8sinφ · ag4x

The product r9F14 is two unknowns multiplied together. The system is not linear.

Replace the pair by a single unknown (r9F14). The system becomes linear, you solve it, and then recover

r9 = (r9F14) / F14

Press Reveal for the assembled system.

Every arrow you draw is an unknown until proved otherwise. Nine arrows → nine scalars.

Analogy — why the slider reaction has no address

Press a book flat on a table and ask “where does the table push?” The honest answer is everywhere along the contact patch. We collapse that distributed pressure into one equivalent force, and its location — not its direction — is what we do not know. That is exactly what r9 is: the address of the resultant. A wide slider can move its resultant around; a narrow one cannot, which is why real linear guides are given length.

Offset crank-sliders in the wild
  • Internal-combustion engines with a deliberate cylinder offset (“desaxe” layouts) trade a little geometric symmetry for reduced piston side-thrust during the power stroke — that side-thrust is F14 in this analysis.
  • Air compressors and pumps. Here F4 is the gas pressure force on the piston and T2 is what the belt must supply.
  • Press and stamping mechanisms, where the peak of F4 near bottom-dead-centre sizes the whole frame.
The direction of the answer is part of the answer

Nothing in the algebra forces F4 to come out positive. Draw the arrow, solve, and read the sign: positive means the force points the way you drew it; negative means it points the other way and the machine is being driven by its load at that instant. In a press, that is the difference between the motor pushing and the flywheel catching a runaway ram.

Group 2 — sliders, and the reaction-direction trap

The offset crank-slider had a slider on ground. Invert it: now the sliding happens between two moving links. Example 5.2 is the one place in the chapter where Stanišić carries every number through, on purpose — including the gc factors that make an inconsistent unit system so hazardous.

Example 5.2

Inverted crank-slider — find the driving torque, numerically

Crank 2 is driven clockwise by the unknown torque T2. Rocker 4 carries a known counter-clockwise load torque T4 = 150.0 ft·lbf. Block 3 is pinned to the crank at B and slides in the slot of link 4. Instantaneously θ2 = 30°, the machine runs at a constant 60 rpm clockwise, so θ2 = −2π rad/s and θ2 = 0. Find T2.

fg2 = (−1.500,  2.598) in  ·  fg2′ = (−2.598, −1.500) in

The first two are trivial — G2 and G3 both ride on the crank. Only G4 needs h3.

Step 3 · Accelerations at θ2 = 0

With zero angular acceleration of the crank, every mass-centre acceleration is purely centripetal: a = f′ θ22, and θ22 = 39.478 rad2/s2.

ag2 = (−102.57, −59.22) in/s2
ag3 = (−205.14, −118.44) in/s2
ag4 = (−45.10, −15.07) in/s2
α2 = 0  ·  α3 = α4 = 4.19 rad/s2

Constant crank speed does not mean zero acceleration anywhere else. Link 4 is angularly accelerating at 4.19 rad/s2 even though the motor is running dead steady.

Step 4 · Inertial terms — mind the gc

Mass is in lbm, acceleration in in/s2, force wanted in lbf. Every product needs ÷386.09.

m2ag2x = 3(−102.57) / 386.09 = −0.80 lbf
m2ag2y = −0.46  ·  m3ag3x = −1.06  ·  m3ag3y = −0.61 lbf
m4ag4x = −0.93  ·  m4ag4y = −0.31 lbf
Ig2α2 = 0  ·  Ig3α3 = 0.0054 in·lbf  ·  Ig4α4 = 2.169 in·lbf

Look at the magnitudes. The load torque is 1800 in·lbf; the largest inertial term is 2.17 in·lbf. This machine is essentially quasi-static.

Step 5 · Free bodies and the moment points
  • Link 2: sum moments about O → F12 drops out.
  • Link 3 (the block): sum moments about G3 → F43 is the only moment left.
  • Link 4: sum moments about A → F14 drops out.

The slot force F43 is perpendicular to the slot, so its direction is known — but where along the block it acts is not, giving the product (r6F43) again.

Unknowns: F12x, F12y, F32x, F32y, F14x, F14y, F43, (r6F43), T2 = 9. Equations: 3×3 = 9. Correct.

Step 6 · Back-substitution beats matrix inversion here

Because of the clever moment points, Equation (5.13) is nearly triangular. Walk it backwards:

row 6 → (r6F43) = I3α3 = 0.0054 in·lbf
row 9 → F43 = 112.81 lbf
rows 4,5 → F32x = −105.53,   F32y = 37.54 lbf

Then row 3 gives T2. Predict its sign before you reveal: the load opposes the crank, so…?

Errata — check the units on the printed answer

The textbook prints T2 = −42.64 in·lbf. Every intermediate value in the book (F43 = 112.81, F32x = −105.53, F12x = 104.73 lbf) reproduces exactly — but they give T2 = 511.7 in·lbf, which is 42.64 ft·lbf. The number is right; the unit label slipped. A useful reflex: the driving torque cannot be 24× smaller than the load torque it is overcoming through a near-unity velocity ratio. Dimensional common sense catches this in two seconds.

Analogy — a bicycle’s dead spot

Push a bicycle crank when it points straight down and nothing happens: the lever arm is zero, so any pedal force gives no torque about the bottom bracket. The inverted crank-slider has the same geometry problem in reverse — there are crank angles where the slot force has almost no moment arm about A, and the required T2 spikes. Slide the crank angle above and find them.

Inverted crank-sliders you have used
  • Shaper and slotter machines. The classic Whitworth quick-return is exactly this mechanism: slow, forceful cutting stroke; fast, light return.
  • Oscillating-cylinder steam engines, where the cylinder itself is the rocking link 4.
  • Aircraft landing-gear retraction, where a hydraulic actuator sliding in a trunnion drives a heavy rocker — and the peak of T2 sizes the hydraulic pump.
Worked examples · 5.3 & 5.4

Group 3 — higher pairs: what a rolling contact actually transmits

A pin joint transmits a force of unknown magnitude and unknown direction. A rolling contact is different: the directions are set by the geometry of the contact, and how many magnitudes are unknown depends on whether the contact is a positive drive or a friction drive. Examples 5.3 and 5.4 are the same machine, drawn twice, differing only in that one modelling choice.

Example 5.3

Rear windshield-wiper drive — positive-drive gears

A motor drives crank 2. The wiper blade is attached to gear 5, which carries a known load torque T5 (aerodynamic drag plus blade friction on the glass). Gears 3 and 5 mesh through a positive drive with a 20° pressure angle; link 4 is the arm joining their centres. Draw the free bodies, then decide whether they are correct by counting the unknowns.

Motor crank 2 → gear 5 sweeps the wiper

Figure 5.32 redrawn. Arm 4 (rust) joins the gear centres and is a two-force member.

Step 1 · The position problem (from Problem 3.7)
r2 + r3 − r4 − r1 = 0

Two scalar components, plus the rolling-contact equation for the mesh:

ρ3Δθ3/4 = −ρ5Δθ5/4
ρ3(θ3−θ3i) + ρ5(θ5−θ5i) − (ρ3+ρ5)(θ4−θ4i) = 0

Three equations, four unknowns (θ2, θ3, θ4, θ5) → one degree of freedom. Solve for the other three by Newton’s method with θ2 given.

Step 2 · Spot the two-force member

Link 4 is pinned at both ends, carries no applied load, and (in this static analysis) has negligible inertia. Therefore the resultant at each pin must be equal, opposite, and collinear with the line joining the pins.

Consequence: F4 has a known direction and only one unknown — its magnitude. And link 4 contributes zero equilibrium equations, because its three equations are already exhausted by the two-force conclusion.

This is where most students lose the count. Recognising a two-force member removes 3 equations and 3 unknowns, then hands back 1.

Step 3 · What the gear mesh transmits

At the pitch point, resolve the tooth force into two components with known directions: Ft35 tangent to the pitch circles, and Fn35 along the line of centres.

Their magnitudes are not independent. The tooth flank is cut at the pressure angle φ, and the resultant must lie along the line of action:

Fn35 = Ft35 tanφ   (5.48)   with φ = 20°

That is one extra scalar equation, and it is the equation that makes the count work out.

Step 4 · The audit

Unknowns (10): |F4| · Fn35 · Ft35 · F15 (2) · F12 (2) · F32 (2) · T2

Equations (10): three each for links 2, 3, 5 = 9, none for the two-force member 4, plus Equation (5.48).

Ten and ten. The free-body diagrams are correct, and T2 is obtainable. T5 is the known load; it sits on the right-hand side.

Answer — ten equations, ten unknowns

The FBDs of Figure 5.33 are correct as drawn. Assemble the nine equilibrium equations for links 2, 3 and 5 together with the tooth relation Fn35 = Ft35tanφ, and solve the 10×10 linear system for

T2,   F12,   F32,   F15,   Fn35,   Ft35,   |F4|

The interesting outputs are not just T2. Ft35 is the tooth load that sets the gear module; Fn35 is the separating force that tries to push the gears apart and is carried entirely by arm 4 and the shaft bearings.

1 / 4
Analogy — a wedge between the teeth

Involute gear teeth do not push each other straight along the tangent. Each tooth flank is a ramp, inclined at the pressure angle. Pushing a crate up a 20° ramp requires a force along the ramp; the ramp pushes back perpendicular to itself, and that perpendicular component is what tries to lift the crate off. In gears, that lifting component is Fn35 = Ft35tan20° ≈ 0.36 Ft35 — about a third of the useful force, spent purely on shoving the shafts apart.

Why the separating force decides the design
  • Wiper motors mount their gear pair in a stamped-steel housing. That housing must resist Fn35 or the gears walk apart and skip teeth on a frozen windshield — exactly when the load torque T5 peaks.
  • High-pressure-angle gears (25°) are stronger in bending but generate ~17% more separating force. That single trade-off runs through every gearbox catalogue.
  • Anti-backlash drives in robotics preload the mesh precisely because Fn reverses when the load reverses, and a loose mesh rattles.
Example 5.4

The same machine with a friction drive

If in Example 5.3 the rolling contact between 3 and 5 had been a friction drive instead of a positive drive, what would the force and moment equilibrium situation have been?

Arm 4 now carries a known preload

Smooth wheels: the arm squeezes them together. That squeeze is designed in, not solved for.

Step 1 · What stays the same

The free-body diagrams are identical. Same bodies, same pins, same two force components at the contact, same two-force member. The arrows do not move.

And the raw count of unknowns is unchanged: ten.

Step 2 · What breaks

Equation (5.48) came from the tooth geometry — the pressure angle of an involute flank. Smooth wheels have no teeth and no pressure angle.

Fn35 = Ft35 tanφ   no longer applies

Ten unknowns, nine equations. Under-determined. So where does the missing information come from?

Step 3 · The preload replaces the geometry

In a friction drive the arm is designed to squeeze the wheels together with a specified force — a spring, a screw, a tensioned link. The force in the arm, F4, therefore becomes a known preload, not an unknown.

Unknowns: 10 − 1 = 9. Equations: 9. Balanced again — by a design decision rather than by geometry.

Answer

The free-body diagrams are unchanged and the unknown count is unchanged, but Equation (5.48) evaporates. Instead, F4 — the force in the arm — is a known preload, reducing the system to nine unknowns and nine equations.

Stanišić adds a practical footnote: realistically a friction drive would not be used in a windshield wiper, and friction drives in general are far less common than positive drives.

Why? Because the transmissible tangential force is capped: Ft35 ≤ μFn35. Exceed it and the drive slips. A positive drive has no such ceiling — the teeth simply carry whatever load the material can survive.

The lesson underneath Example 5.4

Two mechanisms can have identical free-body diagrams and still be different problems. What distinguishes them is not the picture but the constitutive relation at the contact: teeth give you Fn = Fttanφ; friction gives you a designed-in normal load and an inequality Ft ≤ μFn that you must check afterwards. Modelling is choosing which equation you are entitled to write.

Where friction drives still win
  • Traction drives / toroidal CVTs (Nissan Extroid, NuVinci hubs): continuously variable ratio, no teeth to index, and the preload is actively managed by a cam that raises clamping force with torque.
  • Paper-handling rollers in printers and ATMs, where slipping is a feature: it protects the mechanism from a jam.
  • Turntables and capstans, where tooth-meshing noise would be intolerable.
  • In every case the engineer must verify Ft ≤ μFn at the worst crank angle, using exactly the Ft the IDP just produced.

Group 4 — two loops, four bodies, and where the method bites back

Nothing in Steps 5 and 6 cares how many loops the mechanism has. Add bodies, add three equations each; add joints, add unknowns. The bookkeeping grows, the method does not change. Then we look at the three ways a careful student still gets a wrong answer.

Problem 5.1

A two-loop machine — twelve unknowns, twelve equations

Link 2 is the input; θ2, θ2, θ2 are known. A known load torque T5 acts on link 5. Assume Steps 1–4 are complete. Complete Steps 5 and 6 and derive the matrix system for the driving torque T2 and all bearing forces. Sum moments about A for link 2, B for link 3, E for link 4, and F for link 5, and say which unknowns each choice eliminates.

One degree of freedom, two closed loops

Two vector loops, three geometric constraints, one input. Link 4 receives a slider force F42 perpendicular to r7.

Answer — the 12×12 system

Eight force equations (two per body) and the four moment equations above. Stacked as Ax = b with

x = [ F12x F12y F32x F32y F34x F34y F54x F54y F15x F15y F42 T2 ]T

The coefficient matrix has exactly one non-zero in the T2 column (row 9, value −1) and one non-zero in the F15 columns (rows 7 and 8). The right-hand side is a column of pure inertial terms plus the known load T5.

Key structural insight: because T2 appears in a single row, once you know F32x, F32y and F42 you can read the driving torque straight out of equation (9). You never need to invert the full matrix to answer the question the customer asked.

Analogy — a chain of IOUs

Each link owes forces to its neighbours. Summing moments about a pin is like refusing to write down the debts owed at that pin: the bookkeeping shrinks, but nothing is lost, because those debts show up in a neighbour’s ledger. A two-loop machine is simply four ledgers instead of three. The audit — twelve entries, twelve accounts — is how you know no debt has gone unrecorded.

Three ways this method still lies to you

1. The line of action goes somewhere impossible

Solve Example 5.1’s numeric version (below) and recover r9 = (r9F14)/F14. You get −0.43 m — forty-three centimetres from the wrist pin, on a slider a few centimetres long. The resultant of the rail reaction is nowhere near the rail.

Nothing is wrong with the algebra. What is wrong is the model: a single equivalent force cannot represent a distributed pressure once the required moment is large relative to the required force. When F14 is small and its moment is not, r9 flies off to infinity.

Problems 5.10 and 5.11 ask you to notice exactly this and discuss it. The honest reading: the rail carries a couple, and a real linear guide needs length — or two bearings — to supply it.

2. The reaction arrow points the wrong way

Fij is the force from i on j. On body i’s free body you must draw −Fij. Flip one sign and the system still solves — it just gives a confidently wrong answer, usually with the driving torque reversed.

The cheap check: after solving, verify that the reaction pair sums to zero across the joint, and that the sign of T2 has the driver opposing the load. If the motor and the load both push the same way, you have a perpetual-motion machine and a sign error.

3. Nothing checks your answer — except the power equation

An inverted matrix always returns something. The only independent verification available before you cut metal is conservation of energy. Chapter 7 formalises it; use it now:

Σ Tdrive·ω + Σ Fload·v   =   Σ miagi·vgi + Σ Igiαiωi

Left side: the net power the drivers and loads put in. Right side: the rate of change of kinetic energy, computed independently from Steps 1–4. If they do not agree, one of your equilibrium equations is wrong — and the power equation does not care which. It is a global check on a local derivation.

The live solver below evaluates both sides every time you move a slider. Watch the residual: it stays at machine zero unless you have found a bug.

One more failure mode: statically indeterminate machines

If a machine has redundant constraints — a four-bar with a fifth pinned link, a slider on two parallel rails — the unknown count exceeds the equation count and no amount of clever moment-taking will fix it. Rigid-body statics cannot answer the question; you need the stiffness of the parts. That is a finite-element problem, not a Chapter 5 problem. The audit tells you which world you are in before you waste an afternoon.

The power check in industry

Every commercial multibody solver reports an energy residual per timestep, and experienced analysts watch it the way pilots watch fuel flow: not because it is the answer, but because a drifting residual is the earliest sign that the model — not the solver — is broken.

Practice

Practice problems — reveal the solution when you are done, not before

Six problems from the end of Chapter 5, ordered roughly by difficulty. Each gives the vector loops and the constraint equations, so Steps 1–4 are handed to you. Your job is Steps 5 and 6: draw the free bodies, audit the count, choose the moment points, write the matrix. Work them on paper first.

How to attempt these

Do not skip the audit. If your unknown count and equation count do not match before you write a single equilibrium equation, stop and redraw. Roughly two-thirds of wrong answers on this material are wrong pictures, not wrong algebra.

Problem 5.2 · rolling contact + slider

The walkable hoist

As link 2 is rotated counter-clockwise by the driving torque T2, its centre rolls up and to the right, driving link 4 down and to the right; a cable on link 4 hoists a known load F4. The loop and rolling-contact equations are

r1 + r3 − r4 = 0     ρ2(θ2−θ2i) − (ρ2+ρ4)(θ3−θ3i) = 0

The load is hoisted at a constant rate, so r4 is the input and r4 = 0. Sum moments about G2 for link 2, and about A for links 3 and 4. Derive the matrix system for T2 and all bearing forces.

Hint. The mass-centre accelerations came out of a kinematic analysis done in the vector-loop coordinate frame. Therefore all your force components must be resolved in that same frame — not in a frame aligned with gravity or with the incline.

Solution outline

  • Bodies: 2, 3, 4 → nine equilibrium equations.
  • Unknowns: the pin/contact reactions between 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, 1–4, plus T2. The rolling contact between 2 and the ground transmits a normal and a tangential (friction) component — two unknowns, because rolling without slipping means friction is whatever it needs to be.
  • Why G2 for link 2: the contact force on the rolling link has an unknown line of action along the contact; summing about the mass centre keeps the moment arms clean and avoids introducing a fourth unknown.
  • Why A for links 3 and 4: it deletes the pin reactions at A from both moment equations at once.
  • Structure of the answer: nine equations in nine unknowns; T2 appears only in link 2’s moment equation, so it is recovered last by back-substitution.
  • Sanity check: the power delivered by T2 must equal the power absorbed by F4 (constant hoist rate → the kinetic energy of link 4 is constant, but links 2 and 3 are still accelerating).
Problem 5.5 · two sliders on ground

An indexing machine

A known load force F4 resists the output. The single loop equation r2 + r3 − r4 − r1 = 0 has scalar knowns r2, r3, r4, θ4, θ1 and unknowns θ2, θ3, r1. The vectors r9 and r10 locate the centrelines of the left and right bearings. Steps 1–4 are complete. Derive the system for T2 and all bearing forces.

Hint. Two bearings means two reaction lines of action are given to you by r9 and r10. That is a gift — it removes the (rF) product that made Example 5.1 nonlinear.

Solution outline

  • Three moving bodies → nine equations.
  • Because the bearing centrelines are specified, each ground reaction is a single unknown magnitude on a known line — not an unknown magnitude plus an unknown position.
  • Count: F12x, F12y, F32x, F32y, F34x, F34y, the two bearing normals, and T2 → nine.
  • Contrast with Example 5.1 directly. There, one bearing with an unspecified line of action forced the (r9F14) trick. Here, two bearings pin the resultant down and the system is linear from the start. This is a design lesson, not an algebra lesson: two short bearings behave better than one long one.
Problem 5.3 · friction drive + gravity

A machine with a friction drive, and one heavy link

Link 2 is rotated clockwise by T2; the centre of link 4 moves right, resisted by a known load F4. Gravity acts at a known angle θg relative to the vector-loop frame. The loop and rolling-contact equations are

r2 + r3 − r5 − r4 − r1 = 0     (r3−r3i) + ρ4(θ5−θ5i) = 0

Link 3 is an order of magnitude heavier than the others. Include the weight of link 3 only. Sum moments about A (link 2), B (link 3), D (link 4). For each moment equation, state which unknowns the choice eliminated.

Hint. A weight force is a known force with a known line of action through G3. It adds terms to the right-hand side; it adds no unknowns. And it must be resolved into the vector-loop frame using θg — that is the only reason θg was given to you.

Solution outline

  • Three bodies → nine equations. Unknowns: F12x, F12y, F32x, F32y, Fn43, Ft43, F14, (r9F14), T2 → nine. The FBDs are likely correct.
  • The friction-drive contact contributes a normal and a tangential component with known directions. Since this is a friction drive there is no tooth relation, and the count still closes because the ground reaction on 4 supplies the (rF) pairing instead.
  • Moments about A (link 2) eliminates F12x, F12y. About B (link 3) eliminates F32x, F32y. About D (link 4) eliminates the contact-force components at D.
  • Gravity: W = m3g(cosθg, sinθg) added to link 3’s force equations, and its moment about B added to link 3’s moment equation.
  • Afterwards, verify the drive does not slip: Ft43 ≤ μFn43. The IDP does not check this for you.
Problem 5.8 · loop + constraint + rolling contact

A machine with a geometric constraint and a mesh

A known load torque T5 acts on link 5; the driving torque T2 is to be found. The position equations are

−r1 + r2 + r3 − r4 − r5 = 0,   θ3 + π/2 − θ4 = 0,   ρ4(θ4−θ4i) − (ρ4+ρ5)(θ5−θ5i) = 0

Four scalar equations, five unknowns (θ2, θ3, r3, θ4, θ5) → one DOF. Steps 1–4 are complete. Derive the matrix system.

Hint. A geometric constraint like θ3 + π/2 = θ4 means links 3 and 4 are welded at a right angle — they are one rigid body kinematically, but you may still choose to free-body them separately. Decide which, then be consistent.

Solution outline

  • Treat 3 and 4 as a single rigid body (they cannot move relative to each other), or as two bodies with an internal weld reaction — both work, and the second one is three extra equations and three extra unknowns that cancel.
  • The mesh between 4 and 5 supplies normal and tangential components with known directions, plus the tooth relation if it is a positive drive.
  • The slider inside the loop (the r3 that varies) transmits a force perpendicular to r3.
  • Choose moment points at the two ground pins and at the mesh centre; T2 then sits alone in the input link’s moment equation.
Problem 5.10 · the odd one

“Do you notice anything odd? Please discuss.”

The machine moves slowly and the known load F3 is an order of magnitude larger than any link weight. The loop r1 + r2 − r3 = 0 has two scalar components with knowns θ1, θ2, r3 and unknowns r1, r2, θ3. Model the force between 1 and 3 as a force with unknown line of action, exactly as F14 was modelled in Example 5.1. Derive the matrix system for T2.

Hint. Do the derivation honestly, solve it, then compute the line of action. Then ask whether the answer can be true of a real part.

Solution outline — and what the “odd” thing is

  • The algebra proceeds exactly as in Example 5.1: replace the product (r4F13) by a single unknown, solve the linear system, then divide to recover the position.
  • The oddity: the recovered line of action lands outside the physical contact — sometimes far outside, sometimes at infinity when the reaction force approaches zero.
  • Why: replacing a distributed contact pressure by one equivalent force is only legitimate if the required resultant moment can be produced by that force acting somewhere on the contact patch. When the required moment is large and the required force small, no such point exists. The contact is really transmitting a couple.
  • The engineering consequence: a single short bearing cannot react a moment. Real designs use a long bushing, two spaced bearings, or an anti-rotation feature. The mathematics is telling you the design, not failing.
  • Problem 5.11 asks the same question about the force between links 2 and 3, and the same discussion applies.
Problem 5.12 · force analysis instead of kinematics

A planetary geartrain ratio, derived from forces

Repeat Example 3.7 — but instead of a kinematic analysis, use a force analysis to determine the train ratio. The ring gear 4 is fixed, the load torque T5 acts on link 5, and the driving torque T2 acts on link 2. Neglect inertia and gravity, since the ratio accounts for neither. Note ρ4 = ρ2 + 2ρ3.

Hint. With inertia and gravity neglected, every body is in static equilibrium and power in equals power out. The ratio you get must be identical to the kinematic one. If it is not, a tooth force has the wrong sign.

Solution outline

  • Free-body the sun (2), a planet (3), the carrier (5), and note the ring (4) is grounded and needs no equation for the ratio.
  • Each mesh transmits a normal and a tangential component; the tangential components are what carry torque.
  • Planet 3 is a two-mesh body with a pin to the carrier — its moment equation about its own centre relates the two tangential tooth forces. With no inertia, they are equal and opposite in moment.
  • Sun: T2 = ρ2 Ft. Carrier: T5 = (ρ2+ρ3) × (the pin force from the planet) … and the pin force is twice the tooth force, because the planet is loaded on both sides.
  • Assemble and you recover the same Ratio as Example 3.7. That agreement is the point of the problem. Kinematics and statics are two views of the same constraint, connected by the power equation — which is precisely the bridge Chapter 7 builds.
Self-check

Eight questions on Steps 5 and 6

Answer before you click. Feedback appears either way.

Question 1

A planar mechanism has four moving links. How many scalar equilibrium equations are available?

Three per moving body — two force components and one moment about the z axis. Four bodies, twelve equations. The joints determine the number of unknowns, not the number of equations. That is exactly why the audit works.
Question 2

In Example 5.1 the unknown (r9F14) is treated as a single unknown. Why?

The moment equation for link 4 contains the product of two unknowns. Substituting a single symbol for the product linearises the system; afterwards you recover r9 = (r9F14)/F14. The count is unchanged — nine either way.
Question 3

Summing moments for link 2 about the ground pin O, rather than about G2, accomplishes what?

Any force whose line of action passes through the moment point contributes zero moment. Both components of F12 act at O, so both vanish. The price is that you must now add the moment of the inertial force m2ag2 about O — a small, known term. Excellent trade.
Question 4

In Example 5.3 the arm (link 4) contributes how many equilibrium equations to the system?

Recognising a two-force member uses up its three equilibrium equations to conclude that the two pin forces are equal, opposite and collinear. What remains is a single unknown — the magnitude — and no further equations. Forget this and your audit will be off by two.
Question 5

Example 5.4 changes the wiper’s positive drive to a friction drive. What happens to the equation count?

Same picture, different physics. The tooth relation Fn = Fttanφ depends on involute geometry that a smooth wheel does not have. In its place, the designer specifies the clamping preload in the arm. Ten unknowns become nine; nine equations remain. The inequality Ft ≤ μFn is a check afterwards, not an equation.
Question 6

A crank turns at constant angular velocity. Which statement is true of the connecting rod?

With θ2 = 0, α3 = h3′θ22. In the numeric Example 5.1 that is 180 rad/s2 at a steady 25 rad/s crank speed. Constant input speed does not mean a quiet machine — it is the second-order kinematic coefficients that shake things apart.
Question 7

You solve an IDP and recover a slider line-of-action r9 = −430 mm on a 50 mm slider. The correct response is:

The algebra is fine. The model — a distributed pressure replaced by one equivalent force — has broken down, because no point on the contact patch can supply the required moment. This is precisely what Problems 5.10 and 5.11 ask you to discuss. Real designs answer it with a long bushing or two spaced bearings.
Question 8

Your 9×9 system solves cleanly, but the power equation shows a residual of 40 W. What does that tell you?

The rate of change of kinetic energy is already on the right-hand side of the power equation, computed independently from Steps 1–4. The residual is the mismatch after accounting for it. In a frictionless rigid-body model the residual must be zero; anything else means a sign, a moment arm, or a missing reaction. The power check is a global test of a local derivation — it will not tell you which equation, only that one of them is wrong.

Reference

Glossary


Inverse dynamics problem (IDP)
Given a completely specified state of motion, find the forces and torques required to produce it. The motion is the input; the forces are the unknowns. The opposite of the forward (or direct) dynamics problem.
State of motion
The set θ2, θ2, θ2 — the input variable together with its first and second time derivatives. Knowing it makes the entire kinematics of a one-DOF machine computable.
Free-body diagram (FBD)
A sketch of one body, isolated from its neighbours, showing every external force and torque acting on it. Wherever a connection was severed, an action–reaction pair appears.
Action and reaction
Fij denotes the force exerted by body i on body j. Newton’s third law requires Fji = −Fij. Consistent labelling is the single largest source of sign errors in this chapter.
Bearing force
The reaction transmitted through a joint. It is the by-product of the IDP that engineers actually use for bearing selection and link stress analysis, often more valuable than the driving torque itself.
Two-force member
A body loaded only at two points, with negligible inertia and no applied torque. Its two forces must be equal, opposite and collinear. It contributes one unknown (a magnitude) and zero equilibrium equations.
Line of action
The infinite line along which a force acts. A slider reaction has a known direction but an unknown line of action, located by a distance such as r9 — which is itself an unknown.
Linearisation of an (rF) product
Replacing the product of two unknowns, e.g. (r9F14), by a single unknown symbol so the equilibrium equations remain linear. After solving, r9 is recovered by division.
Positive drive
A rolling contact that transmits tangential force by tooth engagement (gears, timing belts, chains). The tooth geometry imposes Fn = Fttanφ, an extra scalar equation.
Friction drive
A rolling contact that transmits tangential force by friction alone. The normal force is a designed-in preload (a known), and the transmissible tangential force is capped by Ft ≤ μFn — an inequality checked after the fact, not an equation.
Pressure angle φ
The angle between the common tangent to the pitch circles and the line of action of the tooth force. Standard values are 14.5°, 20° and 25°. It sets the ratio of separating force to transmitted force.
Separating force
The component of tooth force directed along the line of centres, Fn = Fttanφ. It performs no useful work; it is carried entirely by the shafts, bearings and housing.
Rolling-contact equation
The constraint relating the rotations of two bodies in rolling contact: ρ3Δθ3/4 = −ρ5Δθ5/4 for external contact. It is a position equation, not a force equation.
Consistent set of units
A unit system in which the gravitational constant gc = 1: SI (kg, N, m, s) or British Gravitational (slug, lbf, ft, s). Working outside one is an invitation to a factor of 32.174.
gc
The proportionality constant in F = ma/gc. Equals 32.174 ft·lbm/(lbf·s2), or 386.09 in·lbm/(lbf·s2). Unity in SI and BG.
Kinematic coefficient
A derivative of a dependent position variable with respect to the input, e.g. h3 = dθ3/dθ2. Purely geometric — independent of speed. Chapter 4.
Second-order kinematic coefficient
The second derivative, e.g. h3′ = d2θ3/dθ22. It supplies the centripetal part of the acceleration and is why a constant-speed crank still shakes its rod.
Statically indeterminate
A system with more unknown reactions than independent equilibrium equations. Rigid-body statics cannot resolve it; the distribution depends on the elasticity of the parts.
Power equation
The statement that the net external power equals the rate of change of kinetic energy. Used here as an independent check on the equilibrium equations; developed properly in Chapter 7.
Quasi-static
A regime in which inertial terms are negligible compared with applied loads, so the IDP reduces to a statics problem. Example 5.2’s 1800 in·lbf load against a 2.2 in·lbf inertial term is a good illustration.

Objectives recap

Having worked this session, you should be able to:

  • State the six-step procedure for the inverse dynamics problem and identify which steps belong to Chapter 4 and which to Chapter 5.
  • Draw correct free-body diagrams for every link of a planar machine, with consistent action–reaction labelling.
  • Audit the unknown and equation counts, and diagnose a mismatch as a modelling error rather than an algebra error.
  • Choose moment-summation points that eliminate unknowns, and correctly add the moment of the inertial force when the point is not the mass centre.
  • Linearise an (rF) product and recover the line of action afterwards.
  • Distinguish positive-drive from friction-drive rolling contacts and write the correct auxiliary relation for each.
  • Recognise a two-force member and account for it correctly in the audit.
  • Assemble and solve the linear system for the driving torque or force and all bearing reactions.
  • Verify a solution with the power equation, and interpret a physically impossible line of action as a modelling limitation.
  • Work in a consistent set of units, and explain what gc is doing when you do not.
