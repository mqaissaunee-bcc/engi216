Orientation

How to use this problem session

Chapter 1 asked how many inputs a mechanism needs. Chapter 2 asks the harder question: given those inputs, exactly where does every link go? The answer is the position problem, and one disciplined recipe — the vector loop method — sets it up for any planar mechanism, from a four-bar to a front-end loader. Today we run that recipe through all eleven textbook examples, watch each mechanism move, and then actually solve the equations two ways.

Each example works the same way

  • Watch first. Press Play on the animated skeleton to see the motion and the vectors before any algebra.
  • Step through the derivation. Use Next to advance one logical step at a time: assign vectors, write the loop equation, fix a frame, split into scalar components, count unknowns. Ideal for working it on the board together.
  • Reveal the result. On the last step the boxed position equations and the degree-of-freedom count appear. Predict the DOF before you reveal — then check it against Gruebler.
  • Then solve it. Two live tools — a four-bar closed-form solver and a Newton–Raphson lab — take the equations the rest of the way to numbers.

Everything runs offline in a single file — safe to embed in Canvas, project from a laptop with no Wi-Fi, or hand to students to replay.

Analogy — a treasure-map walk

A vector loop is a set of walking directions that must return you to your starting X. “20 paces along link 2, then 36 along link 3, then back along link 4 and the ground.” If you end up anywhere but where you started, the directions are wrong. Closing the loop — ending exactly at the start — is the single geometric fact that ties all the link angles together. Every position equation in this chapter is just that walk, written in x and y.

Where this goes next

Solve the position problem once and you can place a robot’s gripper, predict where an engine piston sits at a given crank angle, or check that a bucket linkage won’t collide with the cab. Differentiate the very same loop equations and you get velocities and accelerations — the entire rest of the course is built on the loop you learn to write today.

The recipe

The five-step vector loop procedure

Stanišić’s Section 2.1 lays out five steps that every example below follows without exception. Learn the five steps once and the only thing that changes from a four-bar to a front loader is bookkeeping.

  1. Assign a vector to each link, running from one pin joint to the next. Name each vector after its link where you can. The vector’s length is a known dimension; its direction θ is either known or an unknown joint variable. A good assignment is one where every vector represents a real dimension or a joint variable.
  2. Write the vector loop equation (VLE). Walk the vectors head-to-tail around a closed loop and set the sum to zero: the walk must return to its start. One independent VLE per independent loop.
  3. Fix a coordinate frame. Put the origin at a stationary pin and align the X axis with the ground vector r̄1 so that θ1 = 0 and the equations simplify.
  4. Take scalar components. Project the VLE onto X and Y. Each vector rₕ∠θₕ contributes rₕcosθₕ to the X equation and rₕsinθₕ to the Y equation. Two scalar equations per VLE.
  5. Count knowns and unknowns. Scalar unknowns minus scalar equations equals the number of inputs you must supply — the degrees of freedom. It must agree with Gruebler.

The one rule that generates every scalar equation

A planar vector of length r at angle θ (measured counter-clockwise from the +X axis) has components (rcosθ, rsinθ). So the moment you have a loop equation, the two scalar position equations write themselves:

∑ ri cosθi = 0   and   ∑ ri sinθi = 0

with a plus sign for a vector taken in its assigned direction and a minus sign for one taken against it. That is the entire mechanical content of steps 2–4.

Analogy — shadows on two walls

cosθ and sinθ are just the shadow a link casts on the floor (X) and on the wall (Y) when lit from the side and from above. “The loop closes” means the floor-shadows of all the links cancel and the wall-shadows cancel, independently. Two shadows, two scalar equations.

Multiloop mechanisms: independent loops and geometric constraints

A mechanism with more than one loop needs one VLE per independent loop. A loop you can build by adding or subtracting loops you already have is not independent — writing it adds no new information. But multiloop mechanisms hide a second subtlety: several vectors may be glued to the same rigid body, so their directions are locked together by a fixed angle. Those relationships — like θ3 + γ = θ7 — are geometric constraints, and they are extra equations you must add before the unknown count is right.

Two ways to finish the job

Setting up the equations is only half the chapter. Closed-form: for the four-bar (and a few others) you can eliminate one unknown and solve a quadratic exactly — that is Example 2.8 and the live solver below. Numerical: for everything messier, Newton’s method drives the residuals to zero by repeated linear corrections — Examples 2.9–2.10 and the Newton lab. You will use both before the day is out.

Why engineers still write the loop by hand

CAD and multibody software solve these loops automatically — but the solver needs you to know how many inputs the device has, which assembly mode it sits in, and why it sometimes refuses to assemble. Every one of those judgments comes straight out of the five steps. The software does the arithmetic; the engineer owns the loop.

Method — setting up a single loop

Group 1 — one loop, written from scratch

The two mechanisms every engineer meets first. Here the whole point is the setup: assign vectors, close the loop, drop to scalar components, and read the DOF straight off the unknown count.

Example 2.1

The four-bar mechanism

A four-bar mechanism (Figure 2.2): four links in a closed loop, joined by four pins, one link grounded. Follow the five steps to derive the position equations and deduce the degrees of freedom.

crank 2 rotates; the loop stays closed

Vectors r̄2+r̄3 reach the same point as r̄1+r̄4 — the loop closes.

Step 1 · Assign vectors

One vector per link, pin to pin: r̄2 along the crank, r̄3 along the coupler, r̄4 along the output link, and r̄1 along the ground. The angles θ2, θ3, θ4 are the joint variables; the lengths are fixed.

Step 2 · Vector loop equation
r̄2 + r̄3 − r̄4 − r̄1 = 0̄

Walk crank→coupler, then back along output→ground, and you return to the start. (Eq. 2.3)

Step 3 · Fix the frame

Origin at the pin between links 1 and 2; align X with r̄1, so θ1 = 0 and r̄1 has only an X component.

Step 4 · Scalar components
r2cosθ2 + r3cosθ3 − r4cosθ4 − r1 = 0
r2sinθ2 + r3sinθ3 − r4sinθ4 = 0

Equations (2.4) and (2.5) — the position equations.

Step 5 · Count

known r1, r2, r3, r4, θ1=0 unknown θ2, θ3, θ4

Three unknowns, two equations → one must be given. Predict the DOF, then reveal.

Answer — F = 1
3 unknowns − 2 equations = 1 DOF

Specify the crank angle θ2 and the coupler and output angles are determined — one driver runs the whole linkage, agreeing with Gruebler. Note the two scalar equations have two solutions for a given θ2: the open and crossed assembly modes. F = 1 · one input

Analogy — a four-person hand-hold

Four people hold hands in a ring. Move one person’s arm (the crank) and everyone else shifts to keep the ring closed — nobody lets go. You only get to command one arm; the rest follow. That is exactly one degree of freedom.

Where you see the four-bar

Windshield-wiper drives, bicycle-brake calipers, locking pliers, the suspension of many cars, and the classic oil-field pumpjack are all four-bars. It is the most-used mechanism in engineering precisely because one motor produces a useful, repeatable path.

Example 2.2

The offset crank-slider

An offset crank-slider (Figure 2.4): the line of action of the wrist pin does not pass through the crank’s ground pin. Derive the position equations and find the DOF.

crank turns; slider 4 reciprocates

r̄1 is the perpendicular offset; r̄4 is the slider position along its rail.

Step 1 · Assign vectors

r̄2 crank, r̄3 connecting rod, then close with two perpendicular vectors: r̄4 horizontal (slider travel) and r̄1 vertical (the constant offset). Using the shortest distance from a point to a line keeps both meaningful.

Step 2 · Vector loop equation
r̄2 + r̄3 − r̄4 − r̄1 = 0̄
Step 3 · Fix the frame

Origin at the crank ground pin; align axes with r̄4 and r̄1, giving θ4 = 0 and θ1 = −π/2.

Step 4 · Scalar components
r2cosθ2 + r3cosθ3 − r4 = 0
r2sinθ2 + r3sinθ3 + r1 = 0

Equations (2.7) and (2.8). The slider distance r4 is itself one of the unknowns.

Step 5 · Count

known r1, r2, r3, θ1=−π/2, θ4=0 unknown θ2, θ3, r4

Answer — F = 1
3 unknowns − 2 equations = 1 DOF

One input — the crank angle θ2 — fixes both the rod angle and the slider position. Notice an unknown can be a length (r4) just as easily as an angle. F = 1 · one input

1 / 5
Analogy — a bike pump

Your hand circles the crank; the piston slides straight in the cylinder. The connecting rod converts the “going around” into “going in and out.” The offset is just mounting the cylinder slightly off the crank’s center line — common in real engines to tune the side-thrust on the piston.

Where you see the crank-slider

Every piston engine and compressor, scotch-yoke pumps, sewing-machine needles, and stamping presses. When the offset is zero it becomes the in-line slider-crank of a car engine; the offset version shows up where packaging or wear considerations nudge the cylinder aside.

Method — multiple loops & constraints

Group 2 — multiloop mechanisms and geometric constraints

More loops, more bookkeeping. Two new ideas carry the whole group: write one VLE per independent loop, and add a geometric constraint for every pair of vectors locked to the same rigid body. Get those right and the DOF count always matches Gruebler.

Example 2.3

A two-DOF three-link mechanism

Figure 2.14 shows a four-link mechanism that Gruebler’s criterion says has two degrees of freedom. Derive the position equations and confirm the DOF from them.

two inputs move independently

r̄5 spans the two body centers — an extra vector, an extra unknown angle.

Step 1 · Assign vectors

Vectors on links 2 and 3 are pin-to-pin as usual. The circular bodies on 2 and 3 give clear center points, so insert r̄5 between those centers. With r̄4 and ground r̄1 the loop closes.

Step 2 · Vector loop equation
r̄2 + r̄5 + r̄3 − r̄4 − r̄1 = 0̄
Step 3–4 · Frame & scalars

Origin at the pin between 1 and 2, X along r̄1 (θ1=0):

r2cosθ2 + r5cosθ5 + r3cosθ3 − r4cosθ4 − r1 = 0
r2sinθ2 + r5sinθ5 + r3sinθ3 − r4sinθ4 = 0

Equations (2.9) and (2.10).

Step 5 · Count

known r2, r5, r3, r4, r1, θ1=0 unknown θ2, θ5, θ3, θ4

Answer — F = 2
4 unknowns − 2 equations = 2 DOF

Two of the four angles must be supplied — a two-input mechanism, matching Gruebler. The extra vector r̄5 introduced the extra unknown angle that bumps the count from one to two. F = 2 · two inputs

1 / 4
Analogy — an Etch A Sketch

Two knobs, two independent motions: one draws horizontally, one vertically, and together they reach any point. A two-DOF mechanism is the same — you need two commands to pin down where everything sits, and neither knob alone is enough.

Where two DOF earns its keep

Pan-tilt camera mounts, two-joint SCARA placement, and excavator boom-plus-stick motions are all two-DOF: two actuators reach a workspace of points rather than a single fixed path. The cost of that freedom is a second motor and a second command.

Example 2.4

A two-loop mechanism (with geometric constraints)

The two-loop mechanism of Figure 2.16, built from loops 1-2-3-4 and 1-4-3-5-6. Derive the position equations and deduce the DOF. This is the mechanism the Newton lab below actually solves.

one input drives both loops

Two loops share link 3 and 4; r̄3, r̄5, r̄7 ride the same body.

Step 1–2 · Two independent loops

Two loops need two VLEs. A third loop (1-2-3-5-6) exists but is just their difference — not independent.

r̄2 + r̄3 − r̄4 − r̄1 = 0̄
r̄8 + r̄6 − r̄5 − r̄7 − r̄4 − r̄1 = 0̄
Step 3–4 · Four scalar equations

From the first loop, Eqs (2.13)–(2.14); from the second, Eqs (2.15)–(2.16) — four equations in all.

r2cosθ2 + r3cosθ3 − r4cosθ4 − r1 = 0
r8cosθ8 + r6cosθ6 − r5cosθ5 − r7cosθ7 − r4cosθ4 − r1 = 0

(plus the two matching sine equations).

The trap · too many unknowns

Seven unknowns (θ2, θ3, θ4, θ6, r5, θ5, θ7) against four equations would say 3 DOF — but Gruebler says 1. What did the equations miss?

Step · Geometric constraints

Vectors r̄3, r̄7, r̄5 are glued to body 3, so their directions are locked together:

θ3 + γ − θ7 = 0
θ5 + π/2 − θ7 = 0

Two more equations → six total. Add γ to the knowns.

Step 5 · Count again

known r1,r2,r3,r4,r6,r7,r8,θ1=0,θ8,γ unknown θ2..7, r5 (7)

Answer — F = 1
7 unknowns − 6 equations = 1 DOF

The two geometric constraints were the missing equations. With them the count drops from a wrong 3 to the correct 1 — the lesson of multiloop analysis. F = 1 · one input

1 / 6
Analogy — folding-ruler segments

On a carpenter’s folding ruler, three little marks printed on one segment always keep their angles to each other — they ride the same stick. Pretending each mark could point anywhere would over-count the ruler’s freedom. Geometric constraints are how we tell the equations that those directions move together.

Why multiloop matters

Backhoes, automotive valve trains, and printing-press feeds are multiloop. Counting their freedom correctly is the difference between sizing one actuator or three — and an over-counted model will never assemble in software.

Example 2.5

A two-loop mechanism with a length constraint

The mechanism of Figure 2.19 — loops 1-2-3-4 and 1-4-5 — including the rarer case where two vector lengths are tied together. Derive the position equations and the DOF.

coupled two-DOF motion

A sliding pair makes r̄7, r̄8 share a fixed total length: r8 + r7 = L.

Step 1–2 · Two loops

Two independent VLEs (a third loop is dependent):

r̄5 + r̄6 + r̄7 − r̄9 = 0̄
r̄9 − r̄8 − r̄4 − r̄3 − r̄10 − r̄2 − r̄1 = 0̄
Step 3–4 · Four scalar equations

Components give Eqs (2.20)–(2.23). Watch the signs: with θ2 = −π/2, the term −r2sinθ2 becomes +r2.

Step · Four geometric constraints

Three angle constraints from vectors sharing bodies, plus one length constraint from the sliding pair:

θ8−θ7+π=0   θ4−θ8+π/2=0   θ7−θ6+π/2=0
r8 + r7 = L

Eqs (2.24)–(2.27): eight equations in all.

Step 5 · Count

Ten scalar unknowns (r2, θ10, θ3, θ4, θ8, θ7, r8, r7, θ6, θ5) against eight equations.

Answer — F = 2
10 unknowns − 8 equations = 2 DOF

A two-input mechanism. The unusual feature here is the length constraint r8+r7=L — geometric constraints aren’t always about angles. Choosing better loops (Fig 2.21) gets the same DOF with fewer equations. F = 2 · two inputs

1 / 5
Analogy — a trombone slide

Two tube lengths share one fixed total: push the slide out and the inner length shrinks by exactly the amount the outer grows. r8 + r7 = L is that same “what one gains the other loses” bookkeeping, written as an equation.

Length constraints in the wild

Telescoping booms, hydraulic-cylinder strokes, and trombone slides all impose a fixed combined length on two segments. Recognizing them as constraints — not free variables — keeps the DOF count honest.

Analogy — pedaling and steering at once

On an elliptical you supply two things: how hard you stride and how the ramp is set. Each is an independent command, and the pedal’s elliptical path is the mechanism’s response to both — two degrees of freedom you feel in your legs.

Designing the feel of a machine

Gym-equipment engineers tune these link lengths to shape the pedal path — flatter for a walking feel, taller for a climbing feel. The position equations are what let them predict that path before any steel is cut.

Analogy — two joysticks on the loader

The operator has two levers: one raises the arms, one curls the bucket. Two commands, two degrees of freedom. The linkage’s job is to turn those two simple pushes into a scooping motion that keeps the load level.

Keeping the bucket level

Loader linkages are designed so that raising the boom keeps the bucket roughly level automatically (“self-leveling”). That behavior falls straight out of these loop equations — engineers solve them across the full lift range to verify the load won’t spill.

Method — solving in closed form

Group 3 — the four-bar, solved exactly

Setting up the equations is half the job; now we solve them. For the four-bar the two scalar equations can be collapsed to a single equation in one unknown and cracked with a quadratic — an exact, instant answer with no iteration.

Example 2.8

Closed-form solution of the four-bar

Given the four link lengths and the crank angle θ2, solve Equations (2.4)–(2.5) in closed form for θ3 and θ4. The solver above runs exactly these steps.

both assembly modes for one θ2

The ± in the quadratic is the open vs. crossed configuration.

Step 1 · Isolate θ3

Move the θ3 terms to one side of (2.4)–(2.5):

r3cosθ3 = −r2cosθ2 + r4cosθ4 + r1
r3sinθ3 = −r2sinθ2 + r4sinθ4
Step 2 · Square and add

Squaring both and adding kills θ3 (since sin²+cos²=1), leaving the input–output equation in θ4 alone (Eq 2.44).

Step 3 · Standard form
A cosθ4 + B sinθ4 = C

A = 2r4(r1−r2cosθ2),   B = −2r2r4sinθ2,   C = r3²−r2²−r4²−r1²+2r2r1cosθ2. (Eqs 2.46–2.48)

Step 4 · Tangent half-angle

Let u4 = tan(θ4/2). Substituting cosθ4=(1−u²)/(1+u²), sinθ4=2u/(1+u²) turns it into a quadratic:

(C+A)u4² − 2B u4 + (C−A) = 0
Step 5 · Solve & back-substitute
u4 = [ B ± √(A²+B²−C²) ] / (C+A)

θ4 = 2 tan⁻¹ u4 (Eq 2.52). Then recover θ3 from Step 1 with atan2. The ± gives the two assembly modes.

Result — an exact formula
θ4 = 2 tan⁻¹( [B ± √(A²+B²−C²)] / (C+A) )

Two roots → two configurations (open / crossed). A negative discriminant means the links can’t close at that θ2 — the linkage jams. Use the solver above to evaluate this for any inputs instantly.

1 / 5
Analogy — two circles crossing

Fix the crank; the coupler’s far end must lie on a circle of radius r3 about the crank tip and a circle of radius r4 about the output pivot. Two circles meet in (at most) two points — the open and crossed assemblies. The ± in the formula is just “which intersection.” No intersection means no assembly.

Why closed form is prized

A closed-form solution evaluates in microseconds with no convergence worries — ideal for real-time control of a fast four-bar or for sweeping thousands of design candidates. When a mechanism admits one, you take it; when it doesn’t, you reach for Newton’s method.

Method — solving numerically

Group 4 — Newton’s method for nonlinear systems

Most position problems have no tidy closed form. Newton’s method handles them all the same way: linearize, correct, repeat. Example 2.9 builds the machinery on a clean algebraic system; Example 2.10 turns it loose on the two-loop mechanism. Run both in the lab above.

Example 2.9

Newton’s method on a 3×3 system

Find the roots of three coupled nonlinear equations using Newton’s method, starting from the deliberately poor guess x̄⁰ = [0, 0, 0]. This is the primer for the mechanism solve that follows.

iterates march toward the root

Residual norm (rust bar) collapses as the estimate nears [1, 2, 3].

Step 1 · The system
x₁²+x₂+x₃−6 = 0
x₁+x₂²+x₃−8 = 0
x₁+x₂+x₃²−12 = 0

Three equations, three unknowns (Eq 2.63). The true root is [1, 2, 3].

Step 2 · The Jacobian

Each entry is ∂fi/∂xj (Eq 2.64):

J = [ [2x₁, 1, 1], [1, 2x₂, 1], [1, 1, 2x₃] ]
Step 3 · The update
x̄ ← x̄ − J⁻¹ f̄(x̄)

Evaluate f̄ and J at the current guess, solve J·Δx = −f̄ for the correction, and add it. Start at x̄⁰ = [0, 0, 0].

Step 4 · First iteration

At [0,0,0]: f̄ = [−6, −8, −12]. Solving gives the first estimate

x̄¹ = [7, 5, 1]

— nowhere near the root yet, but moving.

Step 5 · Iterate

Repeating drives the estimate in: [3.50, 2.61, 3.45] → [1.92, 1.91, 2.91] → [1.24, 1.95, 2.97] → [1.03, 1.99, 3.00]…

Converged — 5 iterations
x̄ → [1, 2, 3]

From a hopeless first guess, five iterations land on the root to four figures. The number of iterations depends only on the convergence tolerance. Run it yourself in the Newton lab, Mode 1.

1 / 5
Analogy — rolling downhill in fog

You can’t see the valley floor, but you can feel the slope under your feet (the Jacobian). Take the step that slope predicts will reach the bottom, then re-check the slope and step again. Each step gets you dramatically closer — that’s the quadratic convergence of Newton’s method.

The workhorse of engineering software

Newton’s method is what powers the “solve” button in kinematics packages, circuit simulators, and finite-element codes. Understanding its one update line demystifies a tool you’ll lean on for the rest of your career.

Example 2.10

Newton’s method on the two-loop mechanism

Solve the position problem of the Example 2.4 mechanism numerically: the six equations (2.13)–(2.18) for the six unknowns, given the crank angle θ2. The lab above runs this live.

rough guess snaps into the real pose

Iteration 0 is a guessed pose; by iteration 4 the loops close.

Step 1 · Stack the equations

The six scalar equations from Example 2.4 — four loop components plus two geometric constraints — become the residual vector f̄ (Eq 2.65). The unknowns are

x̄ = [θ3, θ4, r5, θ5, θ6, θ7]

with the crank angle θ2 given.

Step 2 · The 6×6 Jacobian

Differentiate each residual with respect to each unknown (Eq 2.66). The loop rows hold terms like −r3sinθ3 and r4sinθ4; the constraint rows are constant ±1 entries.

Step 3 · Rough first guess

To show the method’s robustness, start far from the answer:

x̄⁰ = [0°, 90°, 1.5 in, 0°, 0°, 90°]
Step 4 · Iterate

Each step solves J·Δx = −f̄ and updates x̄. The residual norm falls from order 1 to about 10⁻⁹ in four steps.

Converged — 4 iterations
x̄ → [0.346, 1.84, 0.785, 0.695, −0.508, 2.27]

(radians and inches). The rough guess assembles into the true pose — the same answer the closed-form four-bar can’t give for a mechanism this tangled. Drive it in the Newton lab, Mode 2.

Analogy — snapping a model kit together

You hold the parts roughly in place (the guess), then press — and they click into the one configuration where every pin lines up. Newton’s correction is that press: each iteration nudges all six unknowns at once until every loop closes simultaneously.

One solver, any mechanism

The same six-line routine handles a four-bar, a front loader, or a 30-bar machine — only the residual function and Jacobian change. That generality is why numerical position analysis underlies essentially every commercial multibody-dynamics package.

Method — finding a point

Group 5 — locating a point of interest

Solving the loop gives every link’s angle — but often what you really want is the path of one special point: a tool tip, a weld nozzle, a coupler point. Once the position problem is solved, that point is one short vector sum away.

Example 2.11

A point of interest on the coupler

For the offset crank-slider, find the coordinates of a point Q fixed to the coupler (link 3), located off the coupler line by a known length r5 and angle φ (Figure 2.25).

Q traces its coupler curve

Q rides the coupler; its locus is the rust-colored coupler curve.

Step 1 · A vector loop to Q

Q is reached from the origin by going out the crank, then out to Q along the coupler:

r̄q = r̄2 + r̄5

where r̄5 locates Q on the coupler, at angle φ from r̄3.

Step 2 · Components
rqx = r2cosθ2 + r5cos(θ3+φ)
rqy = r2sinθ2 + r5sin(θ3+φ)

Equations (2.67) and (2.68). Note Q’s direction is θ3+φ — it turns with the coupler.

Step 3 · Solve, then evaluate

First solve the crank-slider position problem for θ3 (and r4) at the given θ2. With θ2 and θ3 now known, plug into (2.67)–(2.68) to get Q’s coordinates.

Result — Q’s path
( rqx, rqy ) as θ2 sweeps → the coupler curve

Sweeping the crank traces Q’s locus — a coupler curve. The position problem is solved once; locating any point on any link afterward is a single vector sum. solve once · locate anything

Analogy — a pen taped to a linkage

Tape a pen anywhere on a moving coupler and let the mechanism run: it draws a curve on the paper. Change where you tape it (r5 and φ) and you get a different curve. Coupler curves are how a single rotating input produces remarkably complex, useful paths.

Coupler curves at work

The straight-line portion of a film-advance mechanism, the dwell of a packaging arm, and classic walking-machine legs are all coupler curves — chosen by an engineer who solved exactly these two equations across the crank’s rotation.

Check yourself

Self-check

Six quick questions on the vector loop method. Pick an answer to see whether it’s right and why.

Question 1

A single vector loop equation produces how many scalar position equations?

Each planar vector equation resolves into exactly two scalar equations: the sum of all ricosθi = 0 and the sum of all risinθi = 0.
Question 2

In a multiloop mechanism, a geometric constraint such as θ3 + γ = θ7 is needed because…

When vectors r̄3, r̄5, r̄7 ride the same body, their angles differ by fixed amounts. Without those extra equations the unknown count is too high and the DOF won’t match Gruebler.
Question 3

In the closed-form four-bar solution, the ± in u4 = [B ± √(A²+B²−C²)]/(C+A) corresponds to…

The two roots are the two ways the links can assemble for one crank angle — the open and crossed configurations (geometric inversions). The designer picks the one the real machine uses.
Question 4

If the discriminant A²+B²−C² comes out negative at some crank angle, it means…

A real square root needs a non-negative argument. When it goes negative the two circles never intersect — the coupler can’t reach, so the linkage physically jams at that crank angle.
Question 5

Newton’s update x̄ ← x̄ − J⁻¹f̄ requires, at each step, the…

The Jacobian J holds ∂fi/∂xj. It is the local linear model of how the residuals change, and solving J·Δx = −f̄ gives the correction step.
Question 6

In the offset crank-slider, one of the scalar unknowns is r4, the slider distance. This shows that…

For a sliding pair the vector’s direction is fixed but its length varies, so the magnitude r4 is the joint variable — an unknown length. Hydraulic-cylinder lengths (r2, r5 in the loader) work the same way.

The vector loop method — position analysisENGI 216 · Ch 2

The five steps

  • 1. Assign vectors pin-to-pin; lengths known, angles are joint variables.
  • 2. Write the VLE — one per independent loop; vectors sum to zero around the loop.
  • 3. Fix the frame — origin at a stationary pin, X along r̄1 so θ1=0.
  • 4. Scalar components — project onto X and Y.
  • 5. Count unknowns − equations = DOF (check against Gruebler).
∑ ricosθi = 0    ∑ risinθi = 0

Multiloop rules

  • Independent loops only — a loop that is the sum/difference of others adds nothing.
  • Geometric constraints — vectors on the same rigid body satisfy θa + (fixed) = θb; add one equation each. Lengths can be constrained too (ra+rb=L).

Four-bar closed form (Eqs 2.45–2.53)

A cosθ4 + B sinθ4 = C  →  u4 = (B ± √(A²+B²−C²)) / (C+A)
  • A = 2r4(r1−r2cosθ2)   B = −2r2r4sinθ2   C = r3²−r2²−r4²−r1²+2r2r1cosθ2
  • θ4 = 2 tan⁻¹ u4; then θ3 = atan2(−r2sinθ2+r4sinθ4, −r2cosθ2+r4cosθ4+r1)/r3.
  • ± = open/crossed mode; discriminant < 0 = no assembly.

Newton’s method (Eq 2.61)

x̄k+1 = x̄k − J⁻¹ f̄(x̄k) ,   Jij = ∂fi/∂xj

Use when no closed form exists. Solve J·Δx = −f̄ each step; iterate until |f̄| is below tolerance.

Reference

Glossary


Vector loop
A chain of vectors, one per link, drawn head-to-tail around a closed path in a mechanism so that the chain returns to its starting point.
Vector loop equation (VLE)
The statement that the vectors around a closed loop sum to zero: ∑ r̄i = 0̄. One independent VLE is written for each independent loop.
Position equations
The two scalar equations (X- and Y-components) obtained from each VLE: ∑ ricosθi = 0 and ∑ risinθi = 0.
Joint variable
A quantity that changes as the mechanism moves — usually a vector’s direction θ (a pin angle), or its length r (a slider distance).
Independent loop
A loop that cannot be formed by adding or subtracting other loops already chosen. A mechanism needs one VLE per independent loop; dependent loops add no new information.
Geometric constraint
An extra equation relating quantities locked together by rigidity — e.g. two vectors fixed to the same body keep a constant angle (θa + γ = θb), or two lengths share a fixed sum (ra + rb = L).
Degrees of freedom (from the equations)
Scalar unknowns minus scalar equations. It is the number of inputs that must be supplied to solve the position problem, and it must equal Gruebler’s F.
Four-bar mechanism
Four links closed by four pins, F = 1. Its position problem has a clean closed-form solution (Example 2.8).
Offset crank-slider
A crank, connecting rod, and slider whose travel line is offset from the crank pivot. Closed with two perpendicular vectors: the offset r̄1 and the slider position r̄4.
Assembly mode (circuit / inversion)
One of the distinct configurations a mechanism can occupy for the same input. The four-bar has two — open and crossed — the ± roots of its closed-form quadratic.
Closed-form solution
An explicit algebraic formula for the unknowns, evaluated directly with no iteration. Available for the four-bar and crank-slider.
Input–output displacement equation
The single equation A cosθ4 + B sinθ4 = C relating the four-bar’s output angle to its input, obtained by eliminating the coupler angle.
Tangent half-angle substitution
The trick u = tan(θ/2), giving cosθ = (1−u²)/(1+u²) and sinθ = 2u/(1+u²), which turns A cosθ+B sinθ=C into a quadratic in u.
Newton–Raphson method
An iterative solver for nonlinear systems: x̄ ← x̄ − J⁻¹f̄, repeated until the residual is small. Used when no closed form exists.
Jacobian (J)
The matrix of partial derivatives Jij = ∂fi/∂xj. It is the local linear model of the residuals and drives each Newton correction.
Residual
The vector f̄(x̄) of left-hand sides of the position equations evaluated at the current estimate. It is zero exactly at a solution; its norm |f̄| measures how far off the estimate is.
Convergence tolerance
The threshold on |f̄| (or on the step size) below which Newton’s iteration is declared finished. It, not the mechanism, sets the iteration count.
Point of interest
Any point whose location you want — a tool tip or coupler point. Once the loop is solved, it is found by one extra vector sum (Eqs 2.67–2.68).
Coupler curve
The path traced by a point fixed to a floating (coupler) link as the input sweeps. Designers exploit coupler curves to get complex paths from a single rotating input.
Grashof / non-Grashof
A four-bar is Grashof if the shortest plus longest link ≤ the other two summed, allowing a link to fully rotate; non-Grashof linkages only rock, and jam at angles where the closed-form discriminant goes negative.
