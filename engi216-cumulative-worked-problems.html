How these problems work

Each problem below has two phases that mirror the two halves of ENGI 216:

Phase A · Kinematics   Close the vector loop, then differentiate it once for velocity and twice for acceleration — or, for the gear train, chase the train value. This tells us how every part moves: the angular velocities, angular accelerations, and the acceleration of every mass center.

Phase B · Kinetics (inverse dynamics)   With the motion known, we draw free-body diagrams and apply Newton–Euler (ΣF = m aG and ΣMG = IGα) to every moving link. That yields the pin/tooth forces and the input torque needed to produce exactly that motion.

The one discipline that saves you

Keep every quantity in one consistent unit system before you multiply anything. Every problem here is worked in strict SI (metres, kilograms, newtons, radians). The single most common error on this material is a silent unit slip — the classic in·lbf vs. ft·lbf mistake — that changes an answer by a factor of twelve and passes every “does the algebra look right” check. When in doubt, write the units next to the number.

Sign convention: angles measured CCW from the +x axis; ω and α positive CCW; forces resolved into (x, y) components. Gravity is neglected in the high-speed problems (inertia dominates) and that assumption is stated where it is used.

Problem 1 · Four-bar linkage — vector loop → force analysis

A planar four-bar linkage drives an oscillating output arm. The crank turns at a steady speed. Find the complete state of motion at the instant shown, then the pin forces and the motor torque required to sustain that motion against a resisting load on the output.

Figure 1 — Four-bar linkage, drawn to scale at θ₂ = 60°.

Given

r₁ = 0.30 m (ground O₂O₄) r₂ = 0.10 m (crank) r₃ = 0.26 m (coupler) r₄ = 0.18 m (rocker) θ₂ = 60° ω₂ = 20 rad/s CCW α₂ = 0 m₂,m₃,m₄ = 0.8, 1.5, 1.1 kg uniform slender links (G at midpoints) T₄ = 20 N·m resisting on rocker find θ₃,θ₄, ω's, α's, pin forces, T₂
Analogy — four people holding hands in a ring

Four people stand in a loop holding hands and nobody lets go. Move one person’s arm (the crank) and everyone else must shift to keep the ring closed. You command exactly one arm; the other three positions follow automatically. That single command is the one degree of freedom, and “keep the ring closed” is precisely the vector-loop equation.

Real-world — where this exact linkage shows up

Windshield-wiper drives, oscillating desk fans, the bucket-leveling linkage on a front loader, and locomotive coupling rods are all four-bars. The crank is a steady motor input; the rocker is the useful oscillating output; the coupler ties them together and traces the rich curves that make four-bars so useful.

Phase A Step 1 — Write the loop and substitute the knowns

Walk the pins O₂ → A → B → O₄ → O₂ and add the link vectors head-to-tail. Because you return to the start, they must sum to zero — that is the loop-closure condition:

r2 + r3 = r1 + r4

Project onto the x- and y-axes to get two scalar equations:

r₂cosθ₂ + r₃cosθ₃ = r₁ + r₄cosθ₄     r₂sinθ₂ + r₃sinθ₃ = r₄sinθ₄

Now put in the numbers (lengths in metres, θ₂ = 60°). Everything except θ₃ and θ₄ is known:

x: 0.10·cos60° + 0.26·cosθ₃ = 0.30 + 0.18·cosθ₄ → 0.0500 + 0.26 cosθ₃ = 0.30 + 0.18 cosθ₄ y: 0.10·sin60° + 0.26·sinθ₃ = 0.18·sinθ₄ → 0.0866 + 0.26 sinθ₃ = 0.18 sinθ₄

Two equations, two unknowns — but θ₃ and θ₄ sit inside cosines and sines, so this pair is nonlinear. There is no one-line algebra solution; we iterate.

Phase A Step 2 — Solve the position by Newton–Raphson

Sketch the linkage to read off a sensible starting guess — open configuration, θ₃ ≈ 20°, θ₄ ≈ 90°. Measure how far the loop fails to close by evaluating the two residuals (left side minus right side):

f₁ = 0.0500 + 0.26 cos20° − 0.30 − 0.18 cos90° = −0.00568 m f₂ = 0.0866 + 0.26 sin20° − 0.18 sin90° = −0.00447 m

Both are a few millimetres from zero, so the guess isn’t quite closed. Correct it in one Newton step by solving J·Δ = −f for the angle corrections:

Δθ₃ = +1.05°   Δθ₄ = +2.33°   ⇒   θ₃ ≈ 21.05°, θ₄ ≈ 92.33°

Re-evaluate the residuals at the updated angles; one more pass drives them below 10⁻¹² m — converged:

Positionθ₃ = 21.02°    θ₄ = 92.32°

Check the x-loop: r₂cosθ₂ + r₃cosθ₃ = 0.0500 + 0.2427 = 0.2927 m, and r₁ + r₄cosθ₄ = 0.30 + (−0.0073) = 0.2927 m. They match. ✓

Phase A Step 3 — Differentiate once (velocity)

Differentiate the loop with respect to time. θ₂, θ₃, θ₄ become rates ω₂, ω₃, ω₄; ω₂ = 20 rad/s is known, so ω₃ and ω₄ are the two unknowns. Collect them on the left — the coefficient matrix is the Jacobian J:

[ −r₃sinθ₃ +r₄sinθ₄ ] [ −0.0932 +0.1799 ] J = [ +r₃cosθ₃ −r₄cosθ₄ ] = [ +0.2427 +0.0073 ] J·[ω₃; ω₄] = [ r₂ω₂ sinθ₂ ; −r₂ω₂ cosθ₂ ] = [ 0.10·20·sin60° ; −0.10·20·cos60° ] = [ +1.732 ; −1.000 ]

Solve the 2×2 system (determinant det J = −0.04433):

Angular velocitiesω₃ = −4.342 rad/s    ω₄ = +7.379 rad/s

The rocker turns CCW (ω₄ > 0); the coupler turns CW (ω₃ < 0) at this instant.

Phase A Step 4 — Differentiate twice (acceleration)

Differentiate again. Here is the Chapter 2 payoff: the matrix on the left is the same J — only the right-hand side changes, now carrying the ω² centripetal terms (with α₂ = 0):

J·[α₃; α₄] = [ r₂ω₂²cosθ₂ + r₃ω₃²cosθ₃ − r₄ω₄²cosθ₄ ] [ r₂ω₂²sinθ₂ + r₃ω₃²sinθ₃ − r₄ω₄²sinθ₄ ] r₂ω₂² = 0.10·20² = 40.0 r₃ω₃² = 0.26·4.342² = 4.90 r₄ω₄² = 0.18·7.379² = 9.80 RHS = [ +24.97 ; +26.61 ]

Same factored J, new load — back-substitute:

Angular accelerationsα₃ = +103.8 rad/s²    α₄ = +192.7 rad/s²
Analogy — the slope stays put, only the terrain shifts

Reusing J is like keeping the same map of the hillside for both the “which way is downhill” question and the “how fast does the slope change” question. The geometry sets J; velocity and acceleration are just two different loads applied to it. Factor J once, reuse it — exactly what a numerical solver does.

Phase A Step 5 — Acceleration of each mass center

Work outward from the fixed pivot. The crank tip A has only centripetal acceleration (α₂ = 0):

aA = −ω₂² r2 = −400·(0.05, 0.0866) = (−10.0, −17.32) m/s²

March across the coupler to B with the rigid-body rule aB = aA + α3×r3 − ω₃²r3. Each link’s center G sits at its midpoint, so aG is the average of its two end accelerations:

LinkaGx (m/s²)aGy (m/s²)|aG| (m/s²)
2 crank−10.00−17.3220.0
3 coupler−27.13−22.9235.5
4 rocker−17.13−5.6018.0

These aG values are the whole reason we did Phase A: they set the inertia (mass×acceleration) each link fights in Phase B.

Phase B Step 6 — Count equations vs. unknowns

Three moving links × three planar equations each = 9 equations. Unknowns: the pin forces F12, F32, F43, F14 (8 scalar components) plus the drive torque T₂ = 9 unknowns. Determinate — solvable exactly.

Real-world — the count is the go/no-go gate

Before any solver (MATLAB, ANSYS) will invert a matrix, it checks that the equation count matches the unknown count. Get it wrong and you either have no solution or infinitely many — both useless for choosing a real motor. Counting first is the same discipline a structural engineer runs before trusting a truss.

Phase B Step 7 — Build each link’s inertia force and torque

Before writing the pin-force equations, compute the known right-hand sides they must equal. For every link the net force equals m·aG and the net moment about G equals IG·α. Slender-rod inertias IG = mL²/12:

IG2 = 0.8·0.10²/12 = 6.67×10⁻⁴,   IG3 = 1.5·0.26²/12 = 8.45×10⁻³,   IG4 = 1.1·0.18²/12 = 2.97×10⁻³ kg·m².

Linkm·aGx (N)m·aGy (N)IG·α (N·m)
2 crank−8.00−13.860 (α₂=0)
3 coupler−40.69−34.38+0.877
4 rocker−18.84−6.16+0.572

These nine numbers are the targets. Phase B now finds the pin forces that produce exactly them.

Phase B Step 8 — Newton–Euler on every link

For each link i write ΣFx = m aGx, ΣFy = m aGy, and moments about Gi: ΣMGi = IGiαi. Newton’s third law couples adjacent links (F23 = −F32, etc.). The 20 N·m load enters link 4’s moment equation opposing ω₄. Assemble the 9×9 system and solve it in one matrix inversion.

By hand you would solve link 4 first (it touches the known load), then link 3, then link 2 — each link passes its pin reaction to the next. A solver does all nine at once.

Cross-check ahead

Problem 2 · Offset slider-crank engine — vector loop → gas-force analysis

A single-cylinder engine runs at roughly 1910 rpm. At the crank angle shown, combustion pressure pushes on the piston. Find the piston motion, then the bearing forces and the crank torque produced — the numbers a designer needs to size the crankshaft bearings and read the torque curve.

Figure 2 — Offset slider-crank at θ₂ = 45°; cylinder centreline offset e above the crank axis.

Given

r₂ = 0.05 m (crank) r₃ = 0.18 m (rod) e = 0.02 m (offset) θ₂ = 45° ω₂ = 200 rad/s (≈1910 rpm) α₂ = 0 m₂,m₃,m₄ = 0.9, 0.6, 0.5 kg Fgas = 8000 N (pushes piston toward crank) find piston x,v,a; bearing forces; T₂
Analogy — a bicycle pump turned by a wheel

Circle the crank and the piston shuttles in and out of the cylinder; the connecting rod converts “going around” into “going in and out.” The offset just mounts the cylinder slightly off the crank centreline — real engines do this to tune the side-thrust the piston presses into the cylinder wall.

Real-world — why the acceleration is enormous

At 200 rad/s the piston sees roughly 1600 m/s² — about 160 g. That inertia is why engine parts are made light and why balancing matters: the shaking forces grow with the square of rpm. The wall force we compute is the source of piston-slap noise.

Phase A Step 1 — Position from the loop

Close the loop O₂ → A → B along the crank and rod; the piston pin B lands on the cylinder centreline, a height e above O₂. So r2 + r3 = x i + e j. The y-component contains only θ₃, so solve it first:

sinθ₃ = (e − r₂sinθ₂) / r₃

Substitute (θ₂ = 45°):

sinθ₃ = (0.02 − 0.05·sin45°) / 0.18 = (0.02 − 0.03536) / 0.18 = −0.0853 → θ₃ = −4.89°

With θ₃ known, the x-component gives the piston position directly:

x = r₂cosθ₂ + r₃cosθ₃ = 0.05·cos45° + 0.18·cos(−4.89°) = 0.0354 + 0.1793 = 0.2147 m
Positionθ₃ = −4.89°    x = 0.2147 m (214.7 mm)
Phase A Step 2 — Velocity

Differentiate the loop once. The y-equation carries only ω₃, so it hands over the rod rate directly:

ω₃ = −(r₂cosθ₂·ω₂)/(r₃cosθ₃) = −(0.05·cos45°·200)/(0.18·cos(−4.89°)) = −7.071 / 0.1793 = −39.43 rad/s

The x-equation then gives the piston velocity:

x = −r₂ω₂sinθ₂ − r₃ω₃sinθ₃ = −7.071 − 0.605 = −7.68 m/s
Velocityω₃ = −39.43 rad/s    piston x = −7.68 m/s
Phase A Step 3 — Acceleration

Differentiate once more (α₂ = 0). The y-equation returns α₃; the x-equation returns the piston acceleration:

α₃ = [ r₂ω₂²sinθ₂ + r₃ω₃²sinθ₃ ] / (r₃cosθ₃) = +7752 rad/s²
x = −r₂ω₂²cosθ₂ − r₃(α₃sinθ₃ + ω₃²cosθ₃) = −1574 m/s²
Accelerationα₃ = +7752 rad/s²    piston x = −1574 m/s²

Note the scale: r₂ω₂² = 0.05·200² = 2000 m/s² of centripetal acceleration at the crank pin alone. At 200 rad/s the mechanism lives in a world of thousands of m/s².

Phase A Step 4 — Mass-center accelerations

With every ω and α known, average the end accelerations of each link to get its center G:

aG2 = (−707, −707)   aG3 = (−1494, −707)   aG4 = (−1574, 0) m/s²

The piston (link 4) rides the full x, so its inertia force alone is m₄|a| = 0.5·1574 ≈ 787 N — and that is before the 8000 N gas load is applied.

Phase B Step 5 — Free bodies & the wall force

Unknowns: pin forces F12, F32, F43 (6 components), the cylinder-wall normal N on the piston (1, perpendicular to the bore), and T₂ = 8 unknowns. Equations: crank (3) + rod (3) + piston (2 force equations) = 8. The known 8000 N gas force moves to the right-hand side of the piston equations.

Phase B Step 6 — Inertia forces on each link

Form the known m·aG vector for each link — these are the loads the pin forces plus the 8000 N gas force must balance:

Linkm·aGx (N)m·aGy (N)
2 crank (0.9 kg)−636−636
3 rod (0.6 kg)−896−424
4 piston (0.5 kg)−7870

The gas force (8000 N, pushing the piston toward the crank) enters the piston’s x-equation on the known side. The piston does not rotate, so it contributes force equations only.

Phase B Step 7 — Solve & interpret the sign
Bearing forces & crank torque
|F₁₂| = 5874 N
|F₃₂| = 6375 N
|F₄₃| = 7226 N
wall N = 437 N
T₂ = −253.8 N·m

Read the negative torque carefully. T₂ being negative means the crank does not need driving here — the gas force is doing work on the shaft. This is the power stroke: the engine delivers 253.8 N·m to the flywheel/load at this instant. A positive result would have meant the shaft must be pushed (compression or exhaust stroke). The 6–7 kN pin forces combine gas load and inertia — that is what the big-end bearing must survive.

Real-world — the torque curve

Sweep θ₂ through a full turn and this T₂ traces the engine’s instantaneous torque curve. Its average over the cycle, times rpm, is the shaft power. The wildly varying instantaneous value is exactly why engines need a flywheel to smooth delivery.

Problem 3 · Compound gear train — rolling contact → torque & tooth loads

A motor accelerates a heavy load through a two-stage compound gear train. This is the “rolling contact” front half (Chapter 3) meeting inverse dynamics: the mesh replaces the pin joint, and the tangential tooth load replaces the bearing force. Find the required motor torque and the load carried by each mesh.

Figure 3 — Compound train: gear 2 drives gear 3; gears 3 and 4 share the intermediate shaft; gear 4 drives gear 5 on the load shaft.

Given

N₂,N₃,N₄,N₅ = 20, 60, 18, 54 teeth module = 2 mm α₂ = 150 rad/s² (input) TL = 40 N·m (load torque) J₂ = 0.004, J₃₄ = 0.012, J₅ = 0.006 kg·m² Jload = 0.500 kg·m² find e, output α, motor T₂, tooth loads
Analogy — bicycle gears

Shift to a low gear and your legs spin fast while the wheel turns slowly — but with much more turning force. A compound train stacks two such reductions on one intermediate shaft, so the ratios multiply: two 3:1 stages give 9:1, trading nine turns of the motor for one turn of the load and multiplying torque nine-fold.

Real-world — the reflected-inertia trick

Robot-joint reducers and machine-tool spindles are sized this way. A big load inertia seen through a 9:1 reduction feels 81× smaller at the motor (the square of the ratio). That is why a small servo can whip a heavy arm around — the gearbox hides the inertia.

Phase A Step 1 — Mesh ratios and train value

At each mesh the two pitch circles roll without slipping, so their pitch-line speeds match and the speed ratio is (driver teeth)/(driven teeth). Take the two meshes in turn:

gear 2 → gear 3: ω₃/ω₂ = N₂/N₃ = 20/60 = 1/3 gear 4 → gear 5: ω₅/ω₄ = N₄/N₅ = 18/54 = 1/3

Gears 3 and 4 are keyed to one shaft, so ω₄ = ω₃. Multiply the two stages for the overall train value e. Both meshes are external, so the sign is positive (output turns the same way as input):

e = ω₅/ω₂ = (N₂·N₄)/(N₃·N₅) = (20·18)/(60·54) = 360/3240 = 1/9
Train valuee = 1/9   (9 : 1 reduction, same sense)
Phase A Step 2 — Angular acceleration of every gear

Ratios apply to acceleration just as to speed. With input α₂ = 150 rad/s²:

α₃ = α₄ = α₂·(N₂/N₃) = 150·(1/3) = 50 rad/s² α₅ = α₂·e = 150·(1/9) = 16.67 rad/s²

The output shaft accelerates nine times slower than the motor — the same 9× the ratio trades away in speed it will pay back in torque.

Phase B Step 3 — Effective inertia at the motor

Refer every rotating inertia to the input shaft. A downstream inertia seen through a speed ratio n is multiplied by n² (the reflected-inertia rule). Gears 3–4 spin at 1/3 the motor speed; gear 5 and the load spin at 1/9:

J_eff = J₂ + J₃₄·(N₂/N₃)² + (J₅ + J_load)·(1/9)² = 0.004 + 0.012·(1/9) + 0.506·(1/81) = 0.004 + 0.001333 + 0.006247 = 0.011580 kg·m²

The huge 0.500 kg·m² load inertia shrinks to 0.00617 kg·m² at the motor — divided by 81. That is the reflected-inertia trick doing the heavy lifting.

Phase B Step 4 — Required motor torque

The motor supplies two things: torque to angularly accelerate the reflected inertia, and torque to overcome the steady load reflected through the ratio:

T₂ = J_eff·α₂ + TL·e = (0.011580)(150) + (40)(1/9) = 1.737 + 4.444 = 6.18 N·m
Motor torqueT₂ = 6.18 N·m

Only ~28% of the effort (1.74 N·m) accelerates the drivetrain; ~72% (4.44 N·m) holds against the load. That split shifts if you accelerate harder or lighten the load.

Phase B Step 5 — Tooth (tangential) loads

Take each shaft as a free body in pure rotation (ΣM = Jα) and divide the net torque by the pitch radius r = module·N/2. With module 2 mm: r₂ = 20, r₃ = 60, r₄ = 18, r₅ = 54 mm.

W₂₃ = (T₂ − J₂α₂)/r₂ = (6.181 − 0.004·150)/0.020 = (6.181 − 0.600)/0.020 = 279 N W₄₅ = (W₂₃·r₃ − J₃₄α₃)/r₄ = (279·0.060 − 0.012·50)/0.018 = (16.74 − 0.60)/0.018 = 897 N
Contact forcesW₂₃ = 279 N    W₄₅ = 897 N

Check. On the output shaft: W₄₅·r₅ − TL = 897·0.054 − 40 = 8.43 N·m, and (J₅ + J_load)α₅ = 0.506·16.67 = 8.43 N·m. They match — the analysis is self-consistent. The second mesh carries 3.2× the first mesh’s load, which is why a gearbox’s low-speed stage uses the wider, stronger gears.

Problem 4 · The same four-bar — kinematic coefficients → power equation

Now solve Problem 1’s mechanism a completely different way. Instead of chasing forces link-by-link, we use kinematic coefficients (Chapter 4) and the power equation (Chapter 7) to get the input torque in one shot — and it must land on the same 10.33 N·m. Two independent roads to one answer is the best error-check you have.

Figure 4 — Same four-bar as Problem 1; here we track the geometry-only coefficients rather than the forces.

Given

geometry & masses identical to Problem 1 ω₂ = 20 rad/s, α₂ = 0 T₄ = 20 N·m resisting find h₃,h₄, h₃′,h₄′, then T₂
Analogy — two trails to the same summit

Newton–Euler is the switchback trail: safe, explicit, one force at a time. The power method is the ridge line: faster, fewer steps, but you must trust your footing. Both reach the same peak. Climbing one and checking against the other is how you know you didn’t wander off a cliff in the algebra.

Phase A Step 1 — First-order kinematic coefficients

A kinematic coefficient is a geometry-only velocity ratio, hi = ωi/ω₂ = dθi/dθ₂. It carries no time — it depends only on configuration. Divide Problem 1’s velocities by the input speed ω₂ = 20 rad/s:

h₃ = ω₃/ω₂ = −4.342 / 20 = −0.2171 h₄ = ω₄/ω₂ = +7.379 / 20 = +0.3690
First-order coefficientsh₃ = −0.2171    h₄ = +0.3690
Phase A Step 2 — Second-order coefficients

The second-order coefficient hi′ = d²θi/dθ₂² captures how the ratio itself changes. In general αi = hiα₂ + hi′ω₂²; with α₂ = 0 that reduces to αi = hi′ω₂², so divide Problem 1’s accelerations by ω₂² = 400:

h₃′ = α₃/ω₂² = 103.8 / 400 = 0.2596 h₄′ = α₄/ω₂² = 192.7 / 400 = 0.4817

These reproduce Problem 1’s α₃, α₄ exactly — the coefficients are the acceleration analysis, repackaged so the input speed factors out.

Phase B Step 3 — The power equation

Power in = power stored (rate of change of kinetic energy) + power out. For a one-DOF system with no gravity:

T₂ω₂ = Σi ( mi aGi·vGi + IGiαiωi ) + TLω₄

The left side is the motor’s power input. On the right, the Σ is the rate the links gain kinetic energy, and TLω₄ is the power drained by the load. Every quantity on the right is already known from Phase A, so the only unknown is T₂:

T₂ = [ Σ(m aG·vG + Iαω) + TLω₄ ] / ω₂
Phase B Step 4 — Plug in and cross-check

Evaluate the sum term by term (mi aGi·vGi + IGiαiωi). The crank runs at constant speed, so its kinetic energy is unchanging and its term is exactly zero:

link 2: 0.00 W (constant-speed crank → KE unchanging) link 3: +42.17 W link 4: +16.89 W Σ = +59.06 W (rate of KE gain of the moving links) power drained by the load: TL·ω₄ = 20·7.379 = 147.58 W

The motor must supply both the KE gain and the load power:

T₂ = (59.06 + 147.58) / ω₂ = 206.64 / 20 = 10.33 N·m
Input torque (power method)T₂ = 10.33 N·m  ✓  identical to Problem 1’s Newton–Euler result

The two methods agree to the last digit. The power route never computed a single pin force, yet it produced the same torque — because internal pin forces do no net work (they act on equal-and-opposite velocities) and drop out of an energy balance. That is the whole point of the coefficient/power machinery: when you need only the driving torque, skip the forces entirely.

Real-world — why simulators love this form

Packaging the geometry into coefficients gives a one-line equation of motion, Ieff(θ₂) α₂ + ½(dIeff/dθ₂)ω₂² = T₂ + Qload, with an effective inertia here of Ieff ≈ 0.0143 kg·m². That is the Lagrangian form multibody solvers integrate — far cheaper than re-solving a force system at every time step.

Problem 5 · Inverted slider-crank (quick-return) — vector loop → force analysis

A crank carries a sliding block that runs in a slotted rocker — the heart of a shaper’s quick-return drive. The sliding-inside-a-rotating-link joint brings in a term the other problems didn’t have: Coriolis acceleration. Find the motion, then the crank torque needed against a load on the rocker.

Figure 5 — Inverted slider-crank at θ₂ = 120°; block slides in the slotted rocker, variable length r₃ measured from O₄.

Given

r₂ = 0.12 m (crank) r₁ = 0.20 m (O₂O₄, O₄ below O₂) θ₂ = 120° ω₂ = 10 rad/s CCW α₂ = 0 rocker: L₄ = 0.34 m, m₄ = 1.2 kg block m₃ = 0.25 kg T₄ = 15 N·m resisting on rocker find r₃, ω₄, accelerations, crank T₂
Analogy — walking outward on a spinning merry-go-round

Step outward while the platform turns and you feel shoved sideways — that sideways push is Coriolis. The block does exactly this: it slides along the rocker (r₃ changes) while the rocker rotates. Ignore the 2 r₃ω₄ Coriolis term and your acceleration — and every force after it — is simply wrong.

Real-world — the quick-return machine tool

Shapers and slotting machines use this exact linkage so the cutting stroke is slow and powerful while the return stroke is fast, saving cycle time. The Whitworth quick-return is a close cousin. The oscillating rocker converts steady crank rotation into a stroke that’s deliberately asymmetric in time.

Phase A Step 1 — Position (closed form)

Place O₂ at the origin and O₄ directly below it at (0, −0.20). The variable-length vector r3 runs from O₄ to the block P, and the block sits at the crank tip. So r3 is simply the vector from O₄ to the crank tip:

crank tip P: Pₓ = r₂cosθ₂ = 0.12·cos120° = −0.0600 Pₖ = r₂sinθ₂ = 0.12·sin120° = +0.1039 r₃ = P − O₄ = (−0.0600 − 0, 0.1039 − (−0.20)) = (−0.0600, 0.3039) r₃ = √(0.0600² + 0.3039²) = 0.3098 m θ₃ = atan2(0.3039, −0.0600) = 101.17° (θ₄ = θ₃, the block lies on the rocker)
Positionr₃ = 0.3098 m    θ₃ = θ₄ = 101.17°
Phase A Step 2 — Velocity (two unknowns: r₃ and ω₄)

The block’s velocity has two descriptions that must agree: as the crank tip, vP = ω₂×r2; as a point sliding in the rocker, vP = r₃ u + ω₄×r3. The crank-tip velocity is:

vPx = −r₂ω₂sinθ₂ = −0.12·10·sin120° = −1.039 m/s vPy = +r₂ω₂cosθ₂ = +0.12·10·cos120° = −0.600 m/s

Match components — a 2×2 system whose determinant is r₃ — and solve for the slide rate and the rocker speed:

Velocityr₃ = −0.387 m/s (sliding inward)    ω₄ = +3.666 rad/s
Phase A Step 3 — Acceleration (Coriolis appears)

Differentiate again. Two extra terms now enter because the block both slides and rotates: the centripetal term r₃ω₄² and the Coriolis term 2r₃ω₄. Evaluate them:

centripetal: r₃ω₄² = 0.3098·3.666² = +4.164 m/s² Coriolis: 2·r₃·ω₄ = 2·(−0.387)·3.666 = −2.837 m/s²

Carry these to the right-hand side (same coefficient matrix as the velocity step) and solve for the slide acceleration and the rocker acceleration:

Accelerationr₃ = −7.19 m/s²    α₄ = −3.336 rad/s²

The block acceleration is aP = (6.00, −10.39) m/s²; the rocker’s mass-center acceleration is (1.00, −2.13) m/s². Drop the Coriolis term and both — and every force after them — come out wrong.

Phase B Step 4 — Free bodies with a sliding joint

The block–rocker contact is frictionless sliding, so that force is a single normal N perpendicular to the rocker (no component along the slot). Unknowns: crank pin F12, F23 (4), the slot normal N (1), rocker pin F14 (2), and T₂ = 8. Equations: crank (3) + block (2) + rocker (3) = 8.

Phase B Step 5 — Inertia forces, solve, and verify by power

The inertia loads are modest here (10 rad/s is gentle). Form them first:

block: m₃aP = 0.25·(6.00, −10.39) = (1.50, −2.60) N rocker: m₄aG4 = 1.20·(1.00, −2.13) = (1.20, −2.56) N rocker: IG4α₄ = 0.0116·(−3.336) = −0.039 N·m

Assemble the 8×8 system (with the slot normal N ⊥ to the rocker) and solve:

Forces & crank torque
|F₁₂| = 46.1 N
|F₂₃| = 47.0 N
slot N = 47.9 N
|F₁₄| = 48.7 N
T₂ = +5.44 N·m

Independent check. The power equation T₂ = [Σ(m aG·vG + Iαω) + T₄ω₄]/ω₂ returns 5.44 N·m as well — confirming both the Coriolis-laden kinematics and the force solution. The slot normal (47.9 N) is the force trying to bend the rocker sideways; it is what the slot walls must bear.

Problem 6 · Capstone — motor + gear reduction + slider-crank press

The whole course in one problem. A motor drives a 5:1 reduction, whose output shaft is the crank of a stamping-press slider-crank. At the working instant the ram meets a forming load. Chain it together: gear train (Ch 3) → vector-loop kinematics (Ch 2) → inverse dynamics (Ch 5) → reflect back to the motor. The deliverable is the motor torque a designer would spec.

Figure 6a — Power flow: motor → 5:1 reduction → crankshaft → slider-crank ram.

Figure 6b — The press slider-crank at crank angle θ₂ = 60°; forming force resists the ram.

Given

motor ω = 60 rad/s (constant) reduction 5:1 → crank ω₂ = 12 rad/s α₂ = 0 r₂ = 0.06 m, r₃ = 0.20 m (in-line) θ₂ = 60° m₂,m₃,mram = 1.2, 0.8, 2.5 kg Fwork = 5000 N resisting ram find crank torque, then motor torque
Analogy — a nutcracker near the squeeze

A press is a toggle: as the crank swings the ram toward the end of its travel, a small crank torque produces an enormous ram force — the same way a nutcracker’s force skyrockets as the jaws close. The gearbox multiplies torque; the slider-crank geometry multiplies it again near the bottom of the stroke.

Real-world — mechanical stamping presses

Sheet-metal presses, can seamers, and coining presses are built exactly like this: a modest motor spins a flywheel-and-crank through a reduction, and the slider-crank converts that into tonnes of forming force for a fraction of a second per stroke. Sizing the motor means doing precisely this chain of calculations.

Phase A Step 1 — Gear stage sets the crank speed

The 5:1 reduction slows the motor to the crank and leaves it at steady speed:

ω₂ = ωmotor/5 = 60/5 = 12 rad/s (≈ 114.6 rpm),   α₂ = 0
Phase A Step 2 — Slider-crank kinematics

In-line (offset = 0), so the loop’s y-component gives the rod angle, and the x-component gives the ram position:

sinθ₃ = −(r₂/r₃)sinθ₂ = −(0.06/0.20)·sin60° = −0.2598 → θ₃ = −15.06° x = r₂cosθ₂ + r₃cosθ₃ = 0.06·cos60° + 0.20·cos(−15.06°) = 0.0300 + 0.1931 = 0.2231 m

Differentiating the loop once gives the ram speed, and once more (α₂ = 0) the ram acceleration:

x = −0.720 m/s    x = −3.03 m/s²
Ram motionθ₃ = −15.06°   x = 0.2231 m   x = −0.720 m/s   x = −3.03 m/s²

The ram moves toward the crank (−x) — this is the working stroke — so the 5000 N forming force acts in the +x direction, resisting it.

Phase B Step 3 — Inverse dynamics on the slider-crank

Same 8×8 free-body system as an engine, but the ram’s external load is now the 5000 N forming force instead of gas. Even at constant crank speed the links still accelerate (x = −3.03 m/s²), so inertia terms remain — though they are small next to the 5000 N load. Solving:

Crank-shaft results
|F₁₂| = 5189 N
|F₃₂| = 5188 N
|F₄₃| = 5186 N
guide N = 1349 N
Tcrank = 300.7 N·m

All three pin forces sit near 5.19 kN — essentially the 5000 N forming load passed hand-to-hand up the linkage, with the guide reaction (1349 N) the sideways thrust the ram presses into its ways.

Phase B Step 4 — Reflect back to the motor

At constant speed the drivetrain needs no torque to accelerate itself, so the crank torque simply divides by the ratio (ideal gears):

Tmotor = Tcrank / 5 = 300.7 / 5 = 60.1 N·m
Motor torque requiredTmotor = 60.1 N·m

Power sanity check. Tmotorωmotor = 60.1×60 = 3609 W, and Tcrankω₂ = 300.7×12 = 3609 W. Power is conserved across the ideal gearbox — torque up 5×, speed down 5×, product unchanged. A 60 N·m motor delivers a 5000 N forming punch: the gearbox and the crank geometry did the multiplying.

Method reference card

The two-phase recipeprint-friendly

Phase A — kinematics

Close the loop  →  differentiate once (velocity)  →  differentiate twice (acceleration)

Velocity and acceleration share the same Jacobian J; only the right-hand side changes (accel adds the ω² terms). Factor J once, reuse it. For a Newton position solve, that same J is the iteration matrix — three uses, one matrix.

Phase B — inverse dynamics (Newton–Euler)

ΣF = m aG    ΣMG = IGα

Count first: 3 equations per moving link vs. (2 per pin joint) + (1 per sliding joint) + (1 input torque). Equal counts ⇒ determinate. Adjacent links share equal-and-opposite joint forces (Newton’s third law).

Shortcut — the power equation (one-DOF)

Tinωin + Σ(load powers) = Σi( miaGi·vGi + IGiαiωi )

Gives the driving torque without any pin forces — ideal as an independent check. Uses kinematic coefficients hi=ωi/ωin and hi′ via αi=hiαin+hi′ωin².

Gear trains

e = ∏(driver teeth)/(driven teeth)  ·  Jeff = Σ Jk(ratiok)²  ·  Tin = Jeffαin + Tload e

Tooth load at a mesh = (net shaft torque)/(pitch radius). Reflected inertia scales with the square of the speed ratio.

Sign & unit discipline

Angles CCW from +x; ω, α positive CCW. One unit system throughout — SI here. A positive input torque means the source drives the mechanism; a negative one means the mechanism drives the source (e.g., an engine power stroke).

Glossary


Vector loop equation
A statement that the position vectors around a closed kinematic chain sum to zero. Splitting it into x- and y-components yields the scalar equations solved for the unknown positions.
Jacobian (J)
The matrix of partial derivatives of the loop equations. It is the coefficient matrix for both the velocity and the acceleration solve, and the iteration matrix for a Newton position solve — unchanged across all three; only the right-hand side differs.
Kinematic coefficient (first order, h)
A geometry-only velocity ratio hi = dθi/dθin = ωi/ωin. Time-independent; depends only on configuration.
Kinematic coefficient (second order, h′)
hi′ = d²θi/dθin². Relates accelerations by αi = hiαin + hi′ωin².
Inverse dynamics
Given the motion, find the forces and torques that produce it. The opposite of forward dynamics (given forces, find the motion).
Newton–Euler equations
The rigid-body laws ΣF = m aG and ΣMG = IGα, written for each moving link.
Free-body diagram (FBD)
A sketch of one link showing every external force and torque acting on it. The starting point of any force analysis.
Two-force member
A massless link loaded only at two pins; its force must lie along the line joining them. A common simplification that reduces unknowns.
Coriolis acceleration
The 2 rω term that arises when a point slides along a link that is simultaneously rotating. Present in the inverted slider-crank; omitting it corrupts every downstream force.
Train value (e)
The overall speed ratio of a gear train, ωout/ωin = ∏(driver teeth)/(driven teeth), with a sign set by the number of external meshes.
Reflected (effective) inertia
An inertia referred to another shaft, scaled by the square of the speed ratio between them. Lets a whole drivetrain be replaced by one equivalent inertia at the input.
Power equation
An instantaneous energy balance: input power + load power = rate of change of kinetic energy. Yields the driving torque without computing internal pin forces.
Pitch radius
Half the pitch diameter of a gear, r = module × N / 2. The tangential tooth force acts at this radius; ratios are set by pitch diameters, not outside diameters.
Mass moment of inertia (IG)
A body’s resistance to angular acceleration about its mass center. For a uniform slender rod, IG = (1/12)mL².
Dead (limit) position
A configuration where the mechanism momentarily loses the ability to transmit motion or force in the usual way — the Jacobian becomes singular. Presses exploit the huge force multiplication near these points.

What you should now be able to do

  • Close a vector loop and differentiate it for velocity and acceleration, reusing one Jacobian throughout.
  • Compute a gear train’s value and its reflected inertia, and size the driving torque and tooth loads.
  • Translate a velocity/acceleration solution into kinematic coefficients and use the power equation for a fast torque answer.
  • Set up and solve a determinate Newton–Euler force system for every pin, tooth, and slot force in a mechanism.
  • Recognise where Coriolis acceleration enters and why omitting it invalidates the force analysis.
  • Chain a full drivetrain — gears, linkage, and load — to spec a real motor, and check the result by conservation of power.
The habit that matters most

Solve every torque two ways when you can — forces one way, energy the other. Problems 1 and 4 are the same mechanism reaching 10.33 N·m by different roads. When your two roads disagree, you have found a bug; when they agree, you can trust the number.
