Analogy — two people pushing a revolving door

A rolling contact is a promise: the two surfaces touching never slip, so the arc length that rolls past the contact point is the same measured on either body. Picture two people walking a revolving door from opposite sides — each must take exactly the same length of step along the glass, or someone gets a shoulder in the ribs. “No slip” is just “same arc length on both sides,” and that single fact generates every gear-ratio equation in this chapter.

Why this chapter runs the world

Every car transmission, e-bike mid-drive, wind-turbine gearbox, cordless-drill planetary head, clock, and watch is a geartrain built from rolling contacts. Solve the position and ratio problem here and you can predict output speed, output torque, and direction for any of them — and read why a planetary set the size of a coffee mug can do what a foot-wide spur train does.

The recipe

The rolling-contact rule — one equation does it all

A rolling contact is a higher pair: two curved bodies touch and roll without slipping. The no-slip condition says the arc length that passes the contact point is identical on both bodies. Written for the changes in orientation, that becomes the single equation this whole chapter is built on.

The no-slip (arc-length) condition

If body A of radius ρA rolls on body B of radius ρB, the arc rolled on A equals the arc rolled on B: ρA·(arc angle on A) = ρB·(arc angle on B). The angles must be measured relative to the link that carries the contact — the arm. Calling that arm body m, Stanišić writes the rolling-contact equation as

ρA ΔθA/m = ± ρB ΔθB/m

where ΔθA/m = ΔθA − Δθm is the rotation of A relative to the arm. The sign is the only thing that changes between the two kinds of contact:

External contact — minus sign. The two bodies sit outside each other and spin in opposite directions at the contact point: ρAΔθA/m = −ρBΔθB/m. This is Stanišić’s Equation (3.1).
Internal contact — plus sign. One body rolls inside the other; they spin the same way at the contact: ρAΔθA/m = +ρBΔθB/m. This is Equation (3.5).
Assembly configuration. Replace each Δθ with (θ − θi), the change from the assembled starting angle θi. That turns the rate relation into a position equation you can solve.
Analogy — outside lane vs. inside lane

External rolling is two runners passing shoulder-to-shoulder in opposite directions on a track — same speed past each other, opposite headings. Internal rolling is a runner on the inside of a circular wall: both the runner and a mark on the wall sweep the same way. Same arc length either way; the geometry just flips who’s facing where. That flip is the ± sign, and it is exactly what makes a single pair of external gears reverse rotation while an internal (ring) gear does not.

From rolling contact to gear ratio

For a geartrain the bodies are gears and the writing of a vector loop becomes pointless — the loop is just “the fixed center is where it is” (r̄1 − r̄1 = 0̄), which says nothing. The only meaningful equations in a geartrain are the rolling-contact equations. Two facts turn them into ratios:

  • Pitch radius is proportional to tooth number. Gears that mesh share a module, so ρ ∝ N. Every radius ratio ρa/ρb may be replaced by the tooth ratio Na/Nb — the quantity you can actually count.
  • The gear ratio is input over output. R = Δθin/Δθout = ωin/ωout. A negative R means input and output turn in opposite directions; a positive R means the same direction.
ρ ∝ N   →   R = Δθin / Δθout = ωin / ωout

Power, torque, and the two reference frames

Power passes through a geartrain (minus friction) unchanged, so torque scales up exactly as speed scales down: Tout = −R·Tin. The minus sign keeps the bookkeeping honest about direction. And one idea separates the easy trains from the clever ones — the arm:

Fixed-axis (simple/compound) trains. Every gear spins on a stationary shaft, so the arm is ground and Δθm = 0. The rolling-contact equation drops to ρAΔθA = ±ρBΔθB.
Planetary (epicyclic) trains. A planet gear’s shaft rides on a moving carrier, so the arm turns. You must keep the relative terms ΔθA/m. That single complication is what lets a tiny planetary set produce a huge ratio.
Brakes select gears. Ground one element of a multi-element epicyclic with a band brake and a different equation goes to zero — giving a different ratio. Stack a few brakes and you have an automatic transmission.
Why engineers still write rolling-contact equations by hand

A gearbox catalog gives you a ratio, but the catalog can’t tell you which way the output turns, why adding an idler changed the sign, or how a clutch pack picks third gear. Those answers come straight from the ± sign and the arm term you learn today. The CAD model does the arithmetic; the engineer owns the contact equation.

Type 1 — rolling contact inside a linkage

Group 1 — mechanisms with a rolling contact

Start where Chapter 2 left off: a real linkage with pins and a slider, but now one joint is a rolling contact. You still write a vector loop — you just add one rolling-contact equation (and sometimes a geometric constraint) before counting. Watch the ± sign decide everything.

Example 3.1

A five-bar mechanism with external rolling contact

Figure 3.9 shows a five-bar mechanism in which coupler body 4 has a rolling contact with the ground. Link 5, the “arm,” is needed in practice to keep 4 touching 1. Derive the position equations and deduce the degrees of freedom.

arm 5 sweeps; disk 4 rolls on ground

Body 4 (disk ρ4) rolls externally on the ground arc ρ1; arm 5 carries the contact.

Step 1 · Vector loop

Five bodies, one loop. Assign one vector per link and close the loop crank→coupler→disk→arm→ground:

r̄2 + r̄3 − r̄4 − r̄5 + r̄1 = 0̄
Step 2 · Scalar components (θ1=0)
r2cosθ2 + r3cosθ3 − r4cosθ4 − r5cosθ5 + r1 = 0
r2sinθ2 + r3sinθ3 − r4sinθ4 − r5sinθ5 = 0

Equations (3.11) and (3.12) — two position equations, but more unknowns than equations. The rolling contact supplies the missing one.

Step 3 · Rolling-contact equation

Body 4 rolls externally on body 1; the arm is body 5. External rolling is Eq. (3.1) — the minus sign:

ρ4 Δθ4/5 = −ρ1 Δθ1/5

i.e. ρ4(Δθ4 − Δθ5) = −ρ1(Δθ1 − Δθ5).

Step 4 · Ground fixed, add assembly config

Body 1 is fixed, so Δθ1 = 0 and the contact equation simplifies to ρ4(Δθ4 − Δθ5) = ρ1Δθ5. Replacing each Δθ by (θ − θi) gives the third position equation:

ρ4(θ4 − θ4i) − (ρ1+ρ4)(θ5 − θ5i) = 0

Equation (3.13).

Step 5 · Count
known r1, θ1=0, r2, r3, r4, r5, ρ1, ρ4, θ4i, θ5iunknown θ2, θ3, θ4, θ5

Three position equations — (3.11), (3.12), (3.13) — in four unknowns. Predict the DOF before revealing.

Answer — F = 1
4 unknowns − 3 equations = 1 DOF

One input runs the whole device. Notice the rolling radii ρ1, ρ4 and the assembly angles θ4i, θ5i are knowns — they enter the equations alongside the link lengths. Gruebler agrees only once link 5 and the rolling contact are accounted for. F = 1 · one input

Analogy — a pizza cutter on the counter

Roll a pizza cutter along the edge of the counter. Your hand is the arm; the wheel is body 4; the counter edge is the ground. The wheel’s spin is locked to how far your hand travels — you cannot turn the wheel independently. That lock is the rolling-contact equation, and it is why the device has just one degree of freedom even though it looks like it has more parts than a four-bar.

Where you see rolling contacts in a linkage

Cam-and-roller followers, friction-drive turntables, rolling-cam clamps, and traction-drive CVTs all bury a rolling contact inside a linkage. The arm-plus-rolling-disk pattern here is the same one used in planetary gearsets — a planet on a carrier rolling on a sun — which is exactly where this chapter is heading.

Example 3.2

A four-link mechanism: a slide plus an internal rolling contact

Figure 3.11 shows a “somewhat strange” four-link mechanism. Body 3 slides in and out of body 4, and body 4 rolls on body 1 — this time on the inside. Derive the position equations and deduce the degrees of freedom.

disk 4 rolls inside ring 1; link 3 slides

Body 4 rolls internally inside the ground ring ρ1; body 3 slides within body 4 (r̄3 ⊥ r̄4).

Step 1 · Vector loop
r̄2 + r̄3 + r̄4 − r̄5 − r̄1 = 0̄

r̄5 is the arm from the ring center to the disk center; r̄3 is the variable-length slide inside body 4.

Step 2 · Scalar components (θ1=0)
r2cosθ2 + r3cosθ3 + r4cosθ4 − r5cosθ5 − r1 = 0
r2sinθ2 + r3sinθ3 + r4sinθ4 − r5sinθ5 = 0

Equations (3.14) and (3.15).

Step 3 · Geometric constraint

Because body 3 slides inside body 4, the slide direction r̄3 is perpendicular to r̄4 — a rigid-body angle lock:

θ4 − θ3 + π/2 = 0

Equation (3.16) — one extra equation that costs no new unknown.

Step 4 · Internal rolling contact

Body 4 rolls internally on body 1; arm is body 5. Internal rolling is Eq. (3.5) — the plus sign:

ρ4 Δθ4/5 = +ρ1 Δθ1/5

With Δθ1 = 0 and the assembly configuration, the homogeneous form is

ρ4(θ4 − θ4i) + (ρ1 − ρ4)(θ5 − θ5i) = 0

Equation (3.17). Compare the sign with (3.13): internal contact flips it.

Step 5 · Count
known r1, θ1=0, r2, r4, r5, ρ1, ρ4, θ4i, θ5iunknown θ2, r3, θ3, θ4, θ5

Four equations — (3.14)–(3.17) — in five unknowns (note r3, a length, is unknown). Predict the DOF.

Answer — F = 1
5 unknowns − 4 equations = 1 DOF

The slide adds an unknown length r3 but the perpendicularity constraint (3.16) adds an equation to match it, and the internal rolling contact (3.17) closes the count. One input still runs the device. The only difference from 3.1 is the sign on the rolling term — internal vs. external. F = 1 · one input

1 / 5
Analogy — a coin rolling inside a ring

Spin a coin so it rolls around the inside of a jar lid. The coin and the lid turn the same way at the point they touch — that is internal rolling, the plus sign. Roll the same coin around the outside of a second coin and they turn opposite ways. Same arc length, opposite sign: that one switch is the whole difference between Examples 3.1 and 3.2.

Where internal rolling shows up

Internal (ring) contact is the heart of ring gears in planetary transmissions, cycloidal speed reducers used in robot joints, and Wankel rotary engines, where a triangular rotor rolls inside a housing. Internal meshes are prized because they share load over more teeth and don’t reverse direction.

Type 2 — fixed-axis geartrains

Group 2 — simple, idler, and compound geartrains

Now every gear spins on a stationary shaft, so the arm is ground and the vector loop says nothing. Only the rolling-contact equations carry information. Three examples build the whole fixed-axis story: a single pair, the effect of an idler, and how a compound gear multiplies ratios.

Example 3.3

A single-stage simple geartrain

In the single-stage simple geartrain of Figure 3.13, gear 2 is the input and gear 3 is the output. Derive the position equations, deduce the degrees of freedom, and compute the gear ratio.

gear 2 drives gear 3 the opposite way

External mesh: input and output rotate in opposite directions — a negative ratio.

Step 1 · The loop is meaningless

A geartrain’s only vector runs between the two fixed centers, so the loop equation reduces to r̄1 − r̄1 = 0̄ — no information. From here on, geartrains are analyzed entirely with rolling-contact equations.

Step 2 · Rolling-contact equation

The contact is external, so Equation (3.1) with the arm being the fixed ground 1:

ρ3 Δθ3/1 = −ρ2 Δθ2/1
Step 3 · Ground fixed + assembly

Body 1 fixed ⇒ Δθ1 = 0, giving ρ3Δθ3 + ρ2Δθ2 = 0 (Eq. 3.25). With the assembly configuration:

ρ3(θ3 − θ3i) + ρ2(θ2 − θ2i) = 0
Step 4 · Count
known ρ2, ρ3, θ2i, θ3iunknown θ2, θ3

One position equation, two unknowns. Predict the DOF and the ratio’s sign.

Answer — F = 1, R = −ρ3/ρ2
R = Δθ2/Δθ3 = −ρ3/ρ2 = −N3/N2

2 unknowns − 1 equation = 1 DOF (Eq. 3.26). The ratio is negative: an external pair reverses direction, so Δθ2 and Δθ3 oppose. Because power is conserved, the driving and load torques then point the same way. R = −N3/N2

1 / 4
Analogy — two interlocked bicycle wheels

Press two bike wheels together rim-to-rim and spin one. The other spins backwards, and a smaller wheel whips around faster than a big one. Speed ratio is just size ratio — and because the rims must travel together, the small one always loses the “turns” race in exact proportion to its radius.

Where you see a single pair

A hand-crank pencil sharpener, a manual egg-beater, and the pinion-and-ring-gear final drive of a rear-wheel-drive car are single external pairs. The direction reversal matters: it’s why a car’s ring gear and pinion are arranged to send the driveshaft’s spin the correct way to the wheels.

Example 3.4

A geartrain with an idler gear

A two-stage simple geartrain is shown in Figure 3.15. The input is gear 2 and the output is gear 3, with gear 4 between them. Determine the position equations, the degrees of freedom, and the gear ratio.

idler 4 makes 2 and 3 turn the same way

Two external meshes in series — the idler flips direction twice, so input and output agree.

Step 1 · Two rolling contacts

Both meshes are external, both on fixed shafts (Eq. 3.1, arm fixed):

ρ3Δθ3 = −ρ4Δθ4    and    ρ4Δθ4 = −ρ2Δθ2

Equation (3.27).

Step 2 · Position equations
ρ3(θ3−θ3i) + ρ4(θ4−θ4i) = 0
ρ4(θ4−θ4i) + ρ2(θ2−θ2i) = 0
Step 3 · Count
known ρ2, ρ3, ρ4, θ2i, θ3i, θ4iunknown θ2, θ3, θ4

Two equations, three unknowns → one degree of freedom.

Step 4 · Eliminate the idler

Eliminate Δθ4 between the two equations of (3.27):

ρ3Δθ3 = ρ2Δθ2

Equation (3.28). The idler radius ρ4 has cancelled completely.

Answer — F = 1, R = +ρ3/ρ2
R = Δθ2/Δθ3 = ρ3/ρ2 = N3/N2

Comparing with Example 3.3, the idler gear 4 changes only the sign of the ratio — it makes input and output turn the same way (Eq. 3.29). Its radius and tooth count have no effect on the magnitude. That is the entire job of an idler. R = +N3/N2

1 / 4
Analogy — passing a bucket down a line

An idler is the person in the middle of a bucket brigade. They don’t change how much water arrives — only which hand it comes from. Add one middle person and the bucket arrives on the other side facing the same way; the line’s “throughput” (the ratio) is untouched.

Where idlers earn their keep

Timing-gear trains in engines use idlers to bridge a long center distance and to set rotation sense without changing speed. A reverse-gear idler in a manual transmission is the classic case: slide an extra gear into the train and the output spins backward — same magnitude, flipped sign — and the car backs up.

Example 3.5

A compound geartrain with two stages of reduction

Derive the position equations and the degrees of freedom for the simple geartrain with two stages of reduction in Figure 3.17. Determine the gear ratio if gear 2 is the input and gear 3 is the output.

gears 4 and 5 share a shaft (compound)

Gears 4 & 5 are rigid on one shaft (compound 45). Each stage reduces — the ratios multiply.

Step 1 · The compound gear

Gears 4 and 5 are bolted to the same intermediate shaft, so they share one angle — call the pair body 45. Two external meshes feed it (Eq. 3.1, arm fixed): 2 drives 4, and 5 drives 3.

Step 2 · Two rolling contacts
ρ2Δθ2 = −ρ4Δθ45
ρ3Δθ3 = −ρ5Δθ45

Equation (3.30).

Step 3 · Position equations + count
ρ2(θ2−θ2i) + ρ4(θ45−θ45i) = 0  (3.31)
ρ3(θ3−θ3i) + ρ5(θ45−θ45i) = 0  (3.32)
known ρ2, ρ3, ρ4, ρ5, θ2i, θ3i, θ45iunknown θ2, θ3, θ45

Two equations, three unknowns → F = 1.

Step 4 · Eliminate the compound shaft

Eliminate Δθ45 between (3.30). Unlike the idler, ρ4 and ρ5 are different, so they do not cancel — they multiply into the ratio:

R = Δθ2/Δθ3 = (ρ3ρ4)/(ρ2ρ5)
Answer — F = 1, R = (N3N4)/(N2N5)
R = (ρ3ρ4)/(ρ2ρ5) = (N3N4)/(N2N5)

Equation (3.33). Two external meshes flip the sign twice, so R > 0 — input and output agree. The product form is the payoff of a compound gear: stacking stages multiplies reductions, so large ratios fit in a small box. R = N3N4 / N2N5

1 / 4
Analogy — gearing down a bicycle twice

One chainring-to-cog step gives you some reduction. Now imagine the rear cog were rigidly tied to a second, smaller chainring that drives a third gear — you’d multiply the two reductions. That’s a compound gear: the shared shaft passes the first stage’s output straight into the second stage’s input, and the ratios stack.

Where compound trains live

Clocks (seconds→minutes→hours), the speed-reduction head of a cordless drill, kitchen-mixer drives, and the high-reduction gearboxes on conveyor motors are all compound trains. When a single pair would need an impractically huge gear, engineers split the reduction across compound stages.

Type 3 — from ratio to torque

Group 3 — power, torque, and a real transmission

A gear ratio is only half a design. The other half is what it does to torque. Example 3.6 takes a real manual-transmission gear set, finds its ratio from tooth counts, then converts horsepower and rpm into an output torque — units and all.

Example 3.6

First gear of a manual transmission

Calculate the gear ratio for 1st gear in the manual transmission of Figure 3.19. If the input is 230 hp at 2000 rpm, calculate the transmission’s output torque.

input 4 → countershaft 56789 → output 1

1st gear: input gear 4 and output gear 1 both route through the compound countershaft cluster 56789.

Step 1 · What 1st gear engages

The shift selector 11 engages gear 1, so the output shaft turns with gear 1 (Δθout = Δθ1) and the input shaft drives gear 4 (Δθin = Δθ4). Power flows through the compound countershaft 56789 in two stages.

Step 2 · Stage 1 — gear 4 on gear 5

Gear 4 rolls externally on gear 5 of the countershaft; arm is the fixed case 13 (Eq. 3.1):

ρ4Δθ4 = −ρ5Δθ56789

Equation (3.34).

Step 3 · Stage 2 — gear 9 on gear 1

Gear 1 rolls externally on gear 9 of the same countershaft; arm is the fixed case (Eq. 3.1):

ρ1Δθ1 = −ρ9Δθ56789

Equation (3.35).

Step 4 · Eliminate the countershaft → ratio

Eliminate Δθ56789 between (3.34) and (3.35) and swap radii for teeth:

R = Δθ4/Δθ1 = (ρ5ρ1)/(ρ4ρ9) = (N5N1)/(N4N9)
= (36·35)/(24·17) = 3.088

Equation (3.36).

Step 5 · Power → torque

Power is torque times speed, so Tin = P/ω = 230 hp / (2000 rev/min) = 0.115 hp·min/rev — not yet a torque unit. Convert with 1 hp = 550 ft·lbf/s:

Tin = 0.115 · 550 · 60 / (2π) ≈ 604 ft·lbf

Then Tout = −R·Tin (Eq. 3.37).

Answer — R = 3.088, Tout ≈ −1865 ft·lbf
Tout = −3.088 × 604 ft·lbf ≈ −1865 ft·lbf

First gear multiplies torque about threefold — exactly why you start moving in 1st. The negative sign says the output torque points opposite the input torque (an even number of reversals would flip it back). Speed drops by the same factor: 2000 rpm → about 648 rpm at the output. R = 3.088 · torque ×3.09

1 / 5
Analogy — a long wrench on a stubborn bolt

Gearing down is just choosing a longer wrench. You turn the handle many times (high input speed) to move the bolt a little (low output speed), but the bolt feels enormous torque. The 3.088 ratio is the wrench length; horsepower is how fast you can keep swinging.

Why first gear exists

An engine makes useful power only in a narrow rpm band, but a car needs huge torque to launch from rest and high speed on the highway. The gearbox trades one for the other: 1st gear’s big ratio gives tire-shredding torque to get moving; top gear’s near-1:1 ratio gives speed. Plug 230 hp, 2000 rpm, and the four tooth counts into the calculator above to reproduce this exact result.

Type 4 — the arm moves

Group 4 — planetary (epicyclic) geartrains

Mount a gear’s shaft on a rotating carrier and the arm is no longer ground — you must keep the relative term ΔθA/m. That single change unlocks the planetary set: huge ratios in a tiny, coaxial package. The trick to every planetary problem is to write the rolling contacts relative to the arm.

Example 3.7

A planetary geartrain with the ring fixed

Calculate the gear ratio for the planetary geartrain of Figure 3.22 if the ring gear 4 is fixed, the arm 5 is the output, and the sun gear 2 is the input.

sun spins fast; carrier turns slow; ring fixed

Sun 2 (input), planets 3 on carrier/arm 5 (output), ring 4 fixed. The carrier turns the same way as the sun.

Step 1 · Write contacts relative to the arm

The planet 3 rides on the arm 5, so its rotation is measured relative to the arm. Sun–planet is external; planet–ring is internal. The two rolling-contact equations (3.38)–(3.39) use the relative angles Δθ2/5, Δθ3/5, Δθ4/5.

Step 2 · Eliminate the planet

The planet 3 is just a coupler — eliminate Δθ3/5 between the two contact equations:

−ρ2Δθ2/5 = ρ4Δθ4/5
Step 3 · Hold the ring

The ring is fixed, Δθ4 = 0, so Δθ4/5 = −Δθ5. Expanding Δθ2/5 = Δθ2 − Δθ5:

ρ2Δθ2 = (ρ2 + ρ4)Δθ5
Step 4 · Solve for the ratio
R = Δθ2/Δθ5 = 1 + ρ4/ρ2 = 1 + N4/N2

The “1 +” is the signature of a planetary with the ring fixed — it comes from the arm carrying the planet around.

Answer — R = 1 + N4/N2 ≈ 3.5
R = 1 + N4/N2

Always positive: sun and carrier turn the same way, so the output torque opposes the input torque. With ρ4 ≈ 2.5ρ2 from the figure, R ≈ 3.5. A simple train giving the same ratio would be roughly three times larger — compactness is the whole reason planetaries exist. R = 1 + N4/N2

1 / 4
Analogy — walking on a moving walkway

The planet’s true motion is its spin plus the ride it gets from the carrier — exactly like your speed over the ground is your walking speed plus the airport walkway carrying you. The “relative to the arm” trick just means: first measure how fast you’re walking on the walkway, then add the walkway. Forget to add the walkway and every planetary calculation comes out wrong.

Planetaries you touch every day

Cordless-drill gearheads, the hub gears in a 3-speed bicycle, automatic-transmission gearsets, wind-turbine and helicopter main gearboxes, and the reduction stage in most electric-vehicle drive units are planetary. Coaxial input and output plus load shared over several planets make them compact, balanced, and strong.

Example 3.8

A planetary with a compound planet — positive or negative ratio

The geartrain of Figure 3.23 has gears 2, the compound planet gear 34, and gear 5, plus arm 6. Element 5 is fixed; the input is the arm 6 (Δθin = Δθ6) and the output is gear 2 (Δθout = Δθ2). Determine the gear ratio.

arm 6 drives; compound planet 34; gear 5 fixed

Compound planet 34 meshes both gear 2 and the fixed gear 5; the arm 6 carries it around.

Step 1 · Two contacts, arm is 6

Gear 2 rolls externally with gear 3 (of compound 34); gear 5 rolls externally with gear 4 (of compound 34). Both relative to the moving arm 6 (Eq. 3.1):

ρ2Δθ2/6 = −ρ3Δθ34/6  (3.43)
ρ5Δθ5/6 = −ρ4Δθ34/6  (3.44)
Step 2 · Eliminate the compound planet

Divide to remove Δθ34/6:

(ρ2/ρ3)Δθ2/6 = (ρ5/ρ4)Δθ5/6
Step 3 · Hold gear 5, expand the arm terms

Set Δθ5 = 0 and expand Δθ2/6 = Δθ2 − Δθ6:

(ρ2/ρ3)(Δθ2 − Δθ6) = −(ρ5/ρ4)Δθ6
Step 4 · Solve for R
R = Δθ6/Δθ2 = 1 / (1 − (ρ3ρ5)/(ρ2ρ4)) = 1 / (1 − N3N5/(N2N4))

Equation (3.45). Now look at what the tooth-number product does to the sign.

Answer — R = 1/(1 − N3N5/N2N4)
R = 1 / (1 − N3N5/N2N4)

This one architecture can give a positive or negative ratio just by choosing tooth counts: if N3N5/N2N4 > 1 the ratio is negative (output reverses); if < 1 it is positive and greater than one. For the Model T values this product exceeds 1, so R < 0. That switchability may have motivated the first semiautomatic transmission, the Ford Model T. sign set by N3N5 vs N2N4

Analogy — gearing that can change its mind

Most gear sets commit to a direction. A compound-planet set is like a seesaw balanced near its tipping point: nudge the tooth counts one way and the output spins forward; nudge them the other way and it spins backward. The denominator 1 − (tooth product) is the seesaw — when it crosses zero, the sign flips and the ratio briefly becomes enormous.

The Ford Model T planetary transmission

The Model T (1908–1927) used pedal-operated band brakes on a planetary gearset instead of a sliding-gear manual box — press a pedal, ground an element, get a gear. It was easy to drive precisely because compound-planet sets like this one let one gear cluster deliver low, reverse, and direct drive. Examples 3.9 and 3.10 work the brake-selected ratios directly.

Analogy — the solar-system metaphor that names it

Group 5 — automatic transmissions and a hybrid train

Everything comes together. Ground one element of a multi-element epicyclic with a band brake and you select a gear — the basis of an automatic transmission (3.9, 3.10). Then combine a fixed-axis stage and a planetary stage into one hybrid train and chase the ratio all the way to tooth numbers (3.11).

A note on these three

We mirror the solution structure and the gear-selection idea, then state the final ratios the book derives. Example 3.11 is worked in full from its four contact equations. Example 3.9

Ford Model T — low-gear ratio by band brake

Calculate the gear ratio for the low-gear operating mode of the Ford Model T semiautomatic transmission (Figure 3.24).

band brake B₂ grounds element 7

Low gear: clamping the band brake B2 stops the drum on element 7 — that choice sets the ratio.

Step 1 · The brake makes the choice

A band brake is the gear selector. In low gear, brake B2 is engaged, clamping the drum that rotates with element 7 so it cannot move:

Δθ7 = 0

Equation (3.49). Grounding a different element would select a different gear — this is the heart of an automatic transmission.

Step 2 · Apply it to the train equations

With the three rolling-contact equations (3.46)–(3.48) of this epicyclic, substitute Δθ7 = 0 into (3.47):

ρ4(Δθ345 − Δθ9) = ρ7Δθ9

Equation (3.50).

Step 3 · Eliminate and solve

Eliminate the relative term between (3.46) and (3.50) to get R = Δθ9/Δθ6:

R = 1 / (1 − (ρ3ρ7)/(ρ6ρ4)) = 1 / (1 − N3N7/(N6N4))
Step 4 · Plug in the teeth

Using the tooth numbers from Figure 3.24:

R = 1 / (1 − (27·21)/(27·33)) = 2.75
Answer — R = 2.75 in low gear
R = 1 / (1 − N3N7/N6N4) = 2.75

In low gear the output torque is 2.75× the input torque and points the opposite way. Release B2 and clamp a different brake and the active equation changes, giving high gear or reverse (Problem 3.21 asks for those; reverse comes out negative). Gear selection = which element you ground. low gear R = 2.75

Analogy — pinning one gear to change them all

Think of a lazy-Susan with three stacked trays. Pin any one tray to the table and spinning a second tray drives the third at a fixed ratio. Pin a different tray and the same hardware gives a new ratio. The band brake is the pin; the driver never has to slide gears in and out.

From Model T pedals to modern automatics

The Model T driver pressed a pedal to clamp a band and get low gear — no clutch-and-stick choreography. Modern automatics do the same thing with hydraulically applied clutches and bands controlled by a computer, but the principle is unchanged: hold one element of an epicyclic and you have selected a gear.

Example 3.10

An automatic transmission — low-speed ratio

Calculate the gear ratio for the low-speed condition of the automatic transmission in Figure 3.25.

band brake B₁ grounds element 3

Low speed: band brake B1 grounds element 3; input is 6, output is the compound 58.

Step 1 · Select low speed

From the transmission’s control table, band brake B1 is engaged in low gear, grounding element 3:

Δθ3 = 0

Equation (3.55).

Step 2 · A 1-DOF system

Equation (3.55) together with the four rolling-contact equations (3.51)–(3.54) is a system of five position equations in six unknowns — one degree of freedom, as a transmission must be.

known all ρ (or N), assembly angles, Δθ3=0unknown θ4, θ58, θ6, θ7, θ9, …
Step 3 · Eliminate in two stages

Eliminate the first planet’s relative term from (3.51)–(3.52), enforce (3.55), and you reach Δθ58 = (1 + ρ3/ρ5)Δθ9 (Eqs. 3.56–3.57, 3.60). Repeat for the second planet (3.53)–(3.54) to get Δθ6 = (1 − ρ8ρ3/ρ6ρ5)Δθ9 (Eqs. 3.58–3.59, 3.61).

Step 4 · Form R = Δθ58/Δθ6
R = (1 + ρ3/ρ5) / (1 − ρ8ρ3/(ρ6ρ5)) = (1 + N3/N5) / (1 − N8N3/(N6N5))
Answer — R = 1.72 in low speed
R = (1 + N3/N5) / (1 − N8N3/N6N5) = 1.72

The shared “1 ±” structure of two planetary stages produces a moderate 1.72:1 reduction. Engage a different brake and a different element grounds, giving a higher or direct ratio from the same hardware. This is exactly how a multi-speed automatic realizes several ratios with no sliding gears. low speed R = 1.72

Analogy — a control panel of brakes

Picture a mixing board where each slider grounds one gear element. Push slider B1 and the machine sings “low gear”; push a different slider and it sings “direct.” The gears never move — only which one is held fast changes — and that is why an automatic can shift smoothly under power.

Inside every torque-converter automatic

Classic automatics (the GM Hydra-Matic lineage and countless others) stack two or three planetary sets and apply clutches and bands in patterns to get 3, 4, 6, even 10 speeds. The control logic is a table of which elements to ground; the kinematics of each row is exactly the calculation you just did.

Example 3.11

A hybrid geartrain — simple stage coupled to a planetary stage

In the geartrain of Figure 3.28 the input is the shaft on the left and the output is the shaft on the right. Assuming the tooth count on each gear is known, derive an expression for the gear ratio in terms of tooth numbers.

simple stage (left) + planetary stage (right), coupled by 56

Left: simple stage 27→34→56 (arm = fixed ground 1). Right: planetary stage with arm 56. Element 56 is shared.

Step 1 · Split the train in two

Six elements: 1, 27, 34, 56, 89, 10. Input Δθ27, output Δθ10. Four external rolling contacts. The first two belong to a simple train (arm = fixed ground 1); the last two belong to a planetary train (arm = 56, which rotates). Element 56 is in both.

Step 2 · Simple stage (arm fixed)

34 rolls externally with 27, and with 56, both about the fixed ground (Eq. 3.1, Δθ1=0):

ρ4Δθ34 = −ρ2Δθ27  (3.68)
ρ3Δθ34 = −ρ5Δθ56  (3.69)
Step 3 · Planetary stage (arm = 56)

27 rolls externally with 89, and 89 with 10, both relative to the rotating arm 56:

ρ7Δθ27/56 = −ρ8Δθ89/56  (3.70)
ρ9Δθ89/56 = −ρ10Δθ10/56  (3.71)
Step 4 · Couple through 56, count

Eliminate Δθ34 from (3.68)–(3.69) to relate the stages:

Δθ56 = (ρ4ρ2)/(ρ5ρ3) Δθ27  (3.72)
known all ρ (or N), assembly anglesunknown θ27, θ34, θ56, θ89, θ10

Four equations, five unknowns → F = 1.

Step 5 · Eliminate and solve for R

Eliminate Δθ89/56 from (3.70)–(3.71), expand the relative rotations, and substitute (3.72). Everything collapses to a ratio in tooth numbers.

Answer — F = 1, R in tooth numbers
R = Δθ27/Δθ10 = N10N5N3N8 / (N7N9N5N3 + N10N4N2N8 − N7N4N2N9)

The simple stage feeds the planetary arm through the shared element 56, so the final ratio mixes both stages’ tooth products — the hallmark of a hybrid train. Every term here is a tooth count you can read off the gears. Master this and no geartrain in the chapter is out of reach. F = 1 · hybrid ratio

Analogy — a relay race with a moving baton handoff

The simple stage runs its leg on solid ground and hands the baton to element 56. But 56 is itself running — it is the moving arm of the planetary leg. So the second runner’s speed is measured relative to a teammate already in motion. Add the two motions correctly (the arm term) and the whole relay’s time — the overall ratio — falls out.

Why hybrids of stages exist

Coupling a fixed-axis stage to a planetary stage lets designers hit oddball ratios and packaging constraints a single architecture can’t. Power-split hybrid-vehicle transmissions, some e-bike mid-drives, and aerospace accessory gearboxes blend stage types exactly like this to balance ratio, size, and where the shafts have to come out.

Check yourself

Self-check

Six quick questions on the ideas behind all eleven examples. Pick an answer to see the explanation.

Question 1

A gear rolls on the inside of a ring gear. The rolling-contact equation ρAΔθA/m = ±ρBΔθB/m takes which sign?

Internal rolling (one body inside the other) takes the plus sign — the two surfaces move the same way at the contact (Eq. 3.5). External contacts take the minus sign (Eq. 3.1) and reverse direction.
Question 2

Why don’t we write a vector loop equation for a geartrain?

For a fixed-axis geartrain the centers don’t move, so the loop equation is trivially zero. All the information lives in the rolling-contact equations — that’s the key shift in Chapter 3.
Question 3

You insert an idler gear between the input and output of a simple pair. What changes?

Comparing Examples 3.3 and 3.4: the idler’s radius cancels (Eq. 3.28), so |R| is untouched. Its only job is to flip the direction — one extra external mesh, one extra sign change.
Question 4

In a compound geartrain, why do the intermediate gears’ tooth counts not cancel the way an idler’s does?

An idler is one gear meshing on both sides, so its radius appears top and bottom and cancels. A compound gear is two different gears rigidly joined; the input-side and output-side radii differ, so R = (N3N4)/(N2N5) — the ratios multiply (Eq. 3.33).
Question 5

A planetary set has the ring fixed, sun as input, carrier as output. The ratio is R = 1 + Nring/Nsun. Where does the “1 +” come from?

Because the carrier turns, you keep the relative term ΔθA/m = ΔθA − Δθm. Carrying the planet around contributes the extra “1,” which is precisely why a small planetary gives a big ratio.
Question 6

In the Model T and the automatic transmission (Examples 3.9–3.10), how is a particular gear ratio “selected”?

Clamping a band brake forces one element’s Δθ to zero (Eqs. 3.49, 3.55). That single condition picks which solution the 1-DOF epicyclic settles into — ground a different element, get a different ratio. That is the whole idea of an automatic transmission.

Rolling contacts & geartrainsENGI 216 · Ch 3

The rolling-contact equation (arm = body m)

ρA ΔθA/m = ± ρB ΔθB/m,   ΔθA/m = ΔθA − Δθm
  • External contact → minus sign (Eq. 3.1); surfaces oppose, direction reverses.
  • Internal contact → plus sign (Eq. 3.5); surfaces agree, direction kept.
  • Assembly configuration: replace each Δθ with (θ − θi) to get a position equation.
  • Pitch radius ∝ tooth count: ρ ∝ N, so ρa/ρb = Na/Nb.

Gear ratio, speed, torque

R = Δθin/Δθout = ωin/ωout;   Tout = −R·Tin;   T(ft·lbf) = 5252·P(hp)/n(rpm)

Fixed-axis trains

  • Simple pair (external): R = −Nout/Nin — negative (Ex 3.3).
  • Idler: R = +Nout/Nin — idler flips sign only; its teeth cancel (Ex 3.4).
  • Compound: R = ∏(Ndriven/Ndriver) — stage ratios multiply (Ex 3.5, 3.6).
  • Sign rule: one factor of (−1) per external mesh; internal meshes keep sign.

Planetary (epicyclic) — Willis equation

(ωsun − ωcarrier) = −(Nring/Nsun)(ωring − ωcarrier)
  • Ring fixed, sun in, carrier out: R = 1 + Nring/Nsun (Ex 3.7) — always positive, compact.
  • Compound planet: R = 1/(1 − N3N5/N2N4) — sign set by the tooth product (Ex 3.8).
  • Planet count to fit: Nplanet = (Nring − Nsun)/2 (must be a whole number).

Automatic / hybrid

  • Band brake selects a gear: ground one element (Δθ=0); a different element → a different ratio (Ex 3.9, 3.10).
  • Hybrid train: a fixed-axis stage feeds a planetary stage through a shared element; ratios of both stages combine (Ex 3.11).
Reference

Glossary


Rolling contact
A higher pair in which two curved bodies touch and roll without slipping, so the arc length passing the contact point is equal on both bodies. The chapter’s fundamental joint.
No-slip (arc-length) condition
The statement that ρA|ΔθA| = ρB|ΔθB| — equal arc rolled on each body. Differentiated, it gives the velocity relation ρAωA = ρBωB.
External contact
Two bodies touching on the outside of each other; they rotate in opposite senses at the contact. Rolling-contact equation takes the minus sign (Eq. 3.1).
Internal contact
One body rolling inside another (e.g. a planet inside a ring gear); they rotate the same sense at the contact. Rolling-contact equation takes the plus sign (Eq. 3.5).
Arm (carrier)
The body that carries a rolling contact — the reference link for the relative angles ΔθA/m. For fixed-axis gears the arm is ground; for a planetary set the arm (carrier) rotates.
Assembly configuration
The set of starting angles θi at which a mechanism is put together. Substituting Δθ = θ − θi converts a rate relation into a position equation.
Pitch radius (ρ)
The radius of the imaginary pitch circle on which two gears effectively roll. Meshing gears share a module, so pitch radius is proportional to tooth number, ρ ∝ N.
Tooth number (N)
The count of teeth on a gear. Because ρ ∝ N, every radius ratio in a geartrain can be replaced by a ratio of (countable) tooth numbers.
Module
The ratio of pitch diameter to tooth number (mm/tooth). Gears must share a module to mesh, which is why pitch radius tracks tooth count.
Gear ratio (R)
R = Δθin/Δθout = ωin/ωout. A negative R means input and output turn in opposite directions; |R| > 1 is a speed reduction (and torque increase).
Train value
The product of driven-over-driver tooth ratios for a geartrain, signed by the number of external meshes. Equivalent to R for fixed-axis trains.
Simple geartrain
Gears on fixed parallel shafts, each shaft carrying one gear, meshing in series. Analyzed purely with rolling-contact equations.
Idler gear
A gear meshing on both sides between input and output. It changes only the sign (direction) of the ratio; its tooth count cancels and does not affect the magnitude.
Compound gear
Two (different) gears rigidly fixed to the same intermediate shaft, sharing one angle. Compound trains multiply stage ratios, giving large reductions compactly.
Planetary (epicyclic) geartrain
A geartrain with one or more planet gears whose shafts ride on a rotating carrier (arm), typically around a central sun gear and inside a ring gear. Compact, coaxial, load-sharing.
Sun gear
The central gear of a planetary set, on the main axis; meshes externally with the planets.
Planet gear
A gear that meshes the sun and (often) the ring while its center orbits on the carrier. It behaves as the coupler of the planetary set and is usually eliminated in the algebra.
Ring (annulus) gear
The outer gear of a planetary set with inward-facing teeth; meshes internally with the planets (plus sign).
Carrier
The arm that holds the planet shafts and orbits them about the sun. Its rotation is the “arm term” that gives planetary trains their characteristic 1 ± structure.
Willis equation
The epicyclic speed balance (ωsun − ωcarrier) = −(Nring/Nsun)(ωring − ωcarrier). One equation governs all choices of fixed/input/output member.
Band brake
A friction band that clamps a drum to ground one element of an epicyclic train (forcing Δθ = 0). Engaging different brakes selects different gear ratios — the basis of an automatic transmission.
Hybrid geartrain
A train combining a fixed-axis stage and a planetary stage, coupled through a shared element. Its overall ratio blends the tooth products of both stages (Ex 3.11).
Degrees of freedom (from the equations)
Scalar unknowns minus scalar (position + rolling-contact) equations. It is the number of inputs needed, and must equal Gruebler’s F.
